% ----- 6.1 Cost Simulation -----
\subsection{Cost Simulation}
\label{subsec:cost_sim}

We simulated the annual operating cost of AnimatorSBT across five adoption scenarios,
ranging from a single-studio pilot to full industry adoption.
The simulation uses industry statistics from JAniCA~(2023) and NAFCA~(2024)
for workforce parameters, and gas costs measured on-chain on the Polygon Amoy
testnet (\cref{tab:gas}; 30~gwei gas price, POL = \$0.22, March 2026 rate).

\subsubsection{Parameters}

The simulation assumes the following industry parameters based on public data:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Total animation creators: $\sim$5{,}500 (JAniCA 2023 survey base)
  \item Freelance ratio: 47.3\%~\cite{janica2023survey} to 69.6\%~\cite{janica2019survey}. The
    high-estimate scenario applies the older 2019 ratio to the 2023 creator base
    as an upper bound; this mixes survey years and should be read as a conservative
    ceiling, not a current figure.
  \item Average projects per animator per year: 6
  \item Batch minting with batch size of 10 (default)
  \item Revocation rate: 1\% of issued SBTs
  \item IPFS pinning: Free tier for $\leq$500 files; \$20/month for larger deployments~\cite{pinata2026pricing}
\end{itemize}

The operating cost is dominated by a flat off-chain subscription rather than by
the chain. At full adoption the modelled total is \$266 a year, of which \$240 is
the fixed pinning tier~\cite{pinata2026pricing} and \$26 is on-chain issuance. The
step from the pilot scenario to the early one is caused entirely by crossing the
provider's free-tier file ceiling, not by any change in blockchain cost. This is
the result we regard as transferable: a design that anchors credentials on chain
and stores their content off it will find its binding cost, and its residual
dependency on one paying party, in the off-chain half.

Two qualifications bound what the figure means. It is a marginal cost---on-chain
transactions plus pinning---and excludes what the system actually requires at
scale: the issuance service, a relayer sponsoring gas for every animator
(\cref{subsec:flow}), wallet recovery, the co-signing workflow, integration
maintenance, and the data protection assessment of \cref{subsec:legal}. And
because the relayer subsidises gas, the total is borne by one sponsoring party on
behalf of the whole industry, which is a sustainability and centralisation
question rather than a low price. We present it as evidence that the chain is not
the barrier, not as a cost of ownership. We do not normalise it against industry
revenue, since that revenue accrues to production committees and rights holders
rather than to the animators the system serves.

Because the dollar figures depend on two volatile parameters---gas price and the
POL/USD rate---\Cref{tab:sensitivity} reports the per-mint cost across a range of
each, computed from the mint gas of 195{,}423 (\cref{tab:gas}). Even in the most
adverse cell a single mint costs under \$0.06, so the conclusion that on-chain cost
is not a barrier survives large swings in both.

\subsection{Scalability}
\label{subsec:scalability}

Throughput and storage are not constraints at this scale and we do not dwell on
them: full adoption implies roughly 22{,}962 certificates a year, about 63
transactions a day against a network processing millions~\cite{gangwal2023layer2},
and 11.5~MB of annual metadata. Growth in the market would not change this
conclusion.

One limit does bind, and it is in our own contract rather than the network.
\texttt{tokensOfOwner()} scans every minted token, so its cost grows with the
\emph{cumulative} supply rather than the annual flow; at around 100{,}000 tokens
the call risks exceeding RPC timeout limits, which the projected issuance rate
reaches within a few years of deployment. We therefore treat the prototype's
implementation as demonstration-only, and an owner-to-token mapping or an
off-chain indexer as a launch requirement rather than a later optimisation. We do
not report gas for the mapping variant, as that would require re-measuring a
modified contract.

% ----- 6.3 Requirements Evaluation -----
\subsection{Requirements Satisfaction}
\label{subsec:requirements_eval}

We assess AnimatorSBT against the five requirements
defined in \cref{subsec:formalization}.
We distinguish what the prototype \emph{empirically demonstrates}---the
integrity/existence aspect of R2 and the cost and throughput results of
\cref{subsec:cost_sim,subsec:scalability}---from what it satisfies \emph{by design
but has not yet validated} with users, at scale, or with the unbuilt components
(R1, R4, R5, and the authenticity aspect of R2).

It is worth being explicit about which of these the test suite can speak to, since
a passing suite is easily read as broader evidence than it is. The 60 tests
exercise contract mechanics: that transfers revert through every ERC-721 path,
that only role-holders may mint, that revocation preserves the record, that
co-signing is idempotent and refuses revoked tokens, and that the canonical
serialisation is order-independent. Those bear on the integrity half of R2 and on
nothing else. R1 depends on who adopts the client, R3 on whether anyone keeps
paying to pin the metadata, R4 and R5 on components that are not built, and the
authenticity half of R2 on an attester we do not control. No unit test reaches any
of them, and we do not present the suite as though it did.

Completeness holds by construction. Issuance is triggered by the work log rather
than by studio approval or a credit committee, so an animator's position in the
subcontracting chain does not determine whether they can receive a certificate.
It presupposes that they use the client at all, and it pulls against Sybil
resistance for the reason given in \cref{subsec:trust}.

Verifiability is the qualification that matters. A third party can establish from
the chain that a claim exists, is unaltered and carries a timestamp, without
contacting the studio that issued it. They cannot establish that the work was
done, because the metadata derives from a self-reported log and the evidence hash
is computed over issuer-authored attributes. Anchoring issuance on an accepted
order (\cref{subsec:integration}) puts a commercial act behind the claim, and the
co-signing layer puts a second key behind it, but until that layer is operated by
an attester the issuer does not control, a co-signed record is operator-attested
rather than independently authenticated.

Persistence is split. Token ownership and issuance timestamps last as long as the
network does; the metadata lasts as long as someone pins it. If the subscription
lapses and no other node holds a copy, the on-chain record survives while the
content it points to does not---which is the same off-chain dependency that
dominates the cost.

Portability and minimal friction are properties of a design rather than of a
running system. Both rest on the embedded-wallet layer, which is not built, and
portability is additionally hostage to wallet recovery, which is unsolved: losing
access today would mean losing the portfolio. The friction claim also understates
what the system adds---wallet provisioning, issuance consent, and decisions about
selective disclosure are new work for the animator, and no study has measured how
much.

% ----- 6.4 Limitations -----
\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:eval_limitations}

Four limitations bound what this evaluation shows, beyond the qualifications
already stated against each requirement.

The prototype is on a testnet. Gas transfers faithfully to mainnet because EVM
execution is identical, but the dollar column does not: testnet POL has no market
value, so the USD figures apply a fixed gas price and a fixed token price to
measured gas and exclude mainnet priority and L1 data costs. They are a model.
\Cref{tab:sensitivity} shows the conclusion survives large moves in both
parameters, but the specific figures should be read as projections rather than
observed costs.

The evidence hash establishes less than its presence suggests, for the reason set
out in \cref{subsec:verification}: it is computed over attributes the issuer
authored and stored alongside them, so it witnesses that nothing changed after
minting and nothing about correspondence to anything outside the record. The
serialisation it depends on is now tested; the live pinning path and the
in-browser recomputation are not.

The prototype's status is not unusual for this class of system. Breckenridge et
al.\ open by observing that decentralised verifiable credential systems have seen
limited deployment in practice~\cite{breckenridge2026picreds}, which is a
statement about the field rather than an excuse for one paper; we report a
testnet prototype because that is where the work is, not because deployment was
attempted and abandoned.

No user study has been run. Perceived value, comprehension of what the credential
does and does not prove, and whether studios would co-sign are all unmeasured, and
the cost model assumes uniform adoption where in practice it would be uneven and
shaped by exactly the incentives \cref{subsec:adoption_barriers} argues are
unfavourable.
