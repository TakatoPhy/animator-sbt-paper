% ----- 6.1 Cost Simulation -----
\subsection{Cost Simulation}
\label{subsec:cost_sim}

We model the annual operating cost across five adoption scenarios, from a single-studio
pilot to full industry adoption, using the gas measured in \cref{tab:gas} at 30~gwei and
POL = \$0.22.

\subsubsection{Parameters}

Four industry parameters carry the model, and the first needs care because no
authoritative headcount of Japanese animators is published. Neither JAniCA nor NAFCA
extrapolates its respondents to a population, and the Japan Standard Industrial
Classification's animation-production code is not tabulated separately in the public
census aggregates. We therefore take the base from the only published estimate we
located: the Association of Japanese Animations puts domestic animation creators at just
under 20{,}000, of whom 5{,}500 work in the drawing roles---animation director, layout,
key animation, in-between check and in-betweening~\cite{aja2025creators}. That is AJA's
own top-down estimate, derived from creator counts in end credits and from the headcount
needed to produce the year's
released minutes at each stage, and AJA notes that in-betweening, finishing and
backgrounds are also produced by overseas subsidiaries and overseas orders. We use
5{,}500 as a stated assumption rather than a measurement.

The share working outside employment is taken from JAniCA's two most recent waves,
37.0\% in 2026~\cite{janica2026survey} and 47.3\% in 2023~\cite{janica2023survey}, the
older of the two serving as the ceiling. We do not carry the 2019 figure into the model,
because applying a seven-year-old ratio to a current base would state a range the
surveys do not support. Each animator is
assumed to work on six projects a year, certificates are minted in batches of ten, 1\%
are revoked, and pinning is charged at the provider's free tier below 500 files and
\$20 a month above it~\cite{pinata2026pricing}.

The base is the parameter the conclusion is least sensitive to. Cost scales with
certificates issued, not with the size of the industry, so an error in the headcount
moves the on-chain column proportionally and leaves the cost per certificate unchanged.
What falls with scale is the per-animator figure, and it falls because the pinning
subscription is flat.

A flat off-chain subscription dominates the operating cost, not the chain. In the largest
scenario the modelled total is \$257.61 a year, \$240 of it the fixed pinning
tier~\cite{pinata2026pricing} and \$17.61 on-chain issuance, and the step from the pilot
scenario to the early one comes entirely from crossing the provider's free-tier file
ceiling rather than from any change in blockchain cost. This is the transferable result: a
design that anchors credentials on chain and stores their content off it will find its
binding cost, and its residual dependency on one paying party, in the off-chain half.

Two qualifications bound what the figure means. It is a marginal cost, on-chain
transactions plus pinning, and excludes what the system requires at scale: the issuance
service, a relayer sponsoring gas for every animator (\cref{subsec:flow}), wallet
recovery, the co-signing workflow, integration maintenance, and the data protection
assessment of \cref{subsec:legal}. And because the relayer subsidises gas, one sponsoring
party bears the total on behalf of the whole industry, which is a sustainability and
centralisation question rather than a low price. We offer it as evidence that the chain is
not the barrier, not as a cost of ownership, and do not normalise it against industry
revenue, which accrues to production committees and rights holders rather than to the
animators the system serves.

The dollar figures depend on two volatile parameters, the gas price and the POL/USD
rate, and the conclusion does not. At the baseline of 30~gwei and \$0.22, a mint of
195{,}423 gas (\cref{tab:gas}) costs \$0.0013. Taking POL to \$1.00 at the same gas
price gives \$0.0059; raising the gas price tenfold to 300~gwei at the baseline rate
gives \$0.0129; and the adverse corner of 300~gwei with POL at \$1.00 gives \$0.0586.
On-chain issuance is not a cost barrier anywhere in that range.

\subsection{Scalability}
\label{subsec:scalability}

Throughput and storage are not constraints at this scale and we do not dwell on them:
the largest scenario implies 15{,}606 certificates a year, which is about 43
transactions a day and 7.8~MB of annual metadata. Neither figure
approaches any limit of a general-purpose network, and growth in the market would not
change that.

One limit does bind, and it is in our own contract rather than the network.
\texttt{tokensOfOwner()} scans every minted token, so its cost grows with the
\emph{cumulative} supply rather than the annual flow; at around 100{,}000 tokens
the call risks exceeding RPC timeout limits, which the largest scenario's issuance rate
reaches in about six years and a partial deployment reaches later but still reaches. We therefore treat the prototype's
implementation as demonstration-only, and an owner-to-token mapping or an
off-chain indexer as a launch requirement rather than a later optimisation. We do
not report gas for the mapping variant, as that would require re-measuring a
modified contract.

% ----- 6.3 Requirements Evaluation -----
\subsection{Requirements Satisfaction}
\label{subsec:requirements_eval}

We assess the framework against the five requirements of \cref{subsec:formalization},
distinguishing what the prototype \emph{demonstrates}---the integrity and existence half
of R2, and the cost and throughput results of
\cref{subsec:cost_sim,subsec:scalability}---from what it satisfies \emph{by design and
has not validated} with users, at scale, or with the unbuilt components (R1, R4, R5 and
the authenticity half of R2).

A passing test suite is easily read as broader evidence than it is, so we should say what
it reaches. The 70 tests exercise contract mechanics: that transfers revert through every
ERC-721 path, that only role-holders may mint, that revocation preserves the record, that
co-signing is idempotent and refuses revoked tokens, and that the canonical serialisation
is order-independent. Those bear on the integrity half of R2 and nothing else. R1 depends
on who adopts the client, R3 on whether anyone keeps paying to pin the metadata, R4 and R5
on components that are not built, and the authenticity half of R2 on an attester we do not
control. No unit test reaches any of them.

Completeness holds by construction. The work log triggers issuance rather than studio
approval or a credit committee, so an animator's position in the subcontracting chain does
not determine whether they can receive a certificate. It presupposes they use the client
at all, and it pulls against Sybil resistance for the reason given in
\cref{subsec:trust}.

Verifiability is the qualification that matters. A third party can establish from
the chain that a claim exists, is unaltered and carries a timestamp, without
contacting the studio that issued it. They cannot establish that the work was
done, because the metadata derives from a self-reported log and the evidence hash
is computed over issuer-authored attributes. Anchoring issuance on an accepted
order (\cref{subsec:integration}) puts a commercial act behind the claim, and the
co-signing layer puts a second key behind it, but until that layer is operated by
an attester the issuer does not control, a co-signed record is operator-attested
rather than independently authenticated.

Persistence is split. Token ownership and issuance timestamps last as long as the network
does; the metadata lasts as long as someone pins it. If the subscription lapses and no
other node holds a copy, the on-chain record survives and the content it points to does
not---the same off-chain dependency that dominates the cost.

Portability is also less automatic than putting a record in the holder's hands
suggests. Hesse et al.\ find that importing a reputation from another platform can help
or hurt depending on what the ratings say, so the receiving market does not simply
accept it at face value~\cite{hesse2021portability}, and Kokkodis and Ipeirotis measure
freelance reputation as only partially transferable even under favourable
conditions~\cite{kokkodis2016transferability}. Both study scores
assembled inside one platform, which a certificate anchored to a studio's own
acceptance is not, so we expect the discount to be smaller here---but we have not
measured it, and a record being portable in the technical sense is not the same as
a verifier treating it as portable.

Portability and minimal friction are properties of a design rather than a running system.
Both rest on the embedded-wallet layer, which is not built, and portability is additionally
hostage to unsolved wallet recovery: losing access today would mean losing the portfolio.
The friction claim also understates what the system adds, since wallet provisioning,
issuance consent and decisions about disclosure are all new work for the animator, unmeasured.

% ----- 6.4 Limitations -----
\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:eval_limitations}

Four limitations bound what this evaluation shows, beyond the qualifications stated
against each requirement.

The prototype is on a testnet. Gas transfers faithfully to mainnet because EVM execution
is identical; the dollar column does not, because testnet POL has no market value, so the
USD figures apply a fixed gas price and token price to measured gas and exclude mainnet
priority and L1 data costs. They are a model. The conclusion survives large moves in both
parameters, as the range above shows, but the figures are projections rather than observed
costs.

The evidence hash establishes less than its presence suggests, for the reason set
out in \cref{subsec:verification}. The serialisation it depends on is now tested;
the live pinning path and the in-browser recomputation are not.

The prototype's status is not unusual for this class of system: Breckenridge et al.\ open
by observing that decentralised verifiable credential systems have seen limited deployment
in practice~\cite{breckenridge2026picreds}. That is a statement about the field rather than
an excuse for one paper. We report a testnet prototype because that is where the work is,
not because deployment was attempted and abandoned.

No user study has been run. Perceived value, comprehension of what the credential
does and does not prove, and whether studios would co-sign are all unmeasured, and
the cost model assumes uniform adoption where in practice it would be uneven and
shaped by exactly the incentives \cref{subsec:adoption_barriers} argues are
unfavourable.
