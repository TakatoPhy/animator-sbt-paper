% ----- 7.1 Adoption Barriers -----
\subsection{Adoption Barriers}
\label{subsec:adoption_barriers}

The barriers to this framework are not technical, and the sharpest of them is
internal to the design. Co-signing is what converts a self-report into a
credential, and the transparency it creates is what a studio has reason to avoid:
a countersigned record of who did which cut also documents how deep the
outsourcing chain runs. The party whose participation the credential needs is
therefore the party it exposes.

Anchoring issuance on an accepted order separates two objections that are easily
conflated. One is effort: a studio asked to vouch for work must review it, decide,
and stand behind the decision. That objection largely dissolves, because accepting
a delivery and paying for it \emph{is} the review, and it has already happened by
the time a certificate could be issued; the co-signature is a signature on a
determination the studio made for its own commercial reasons, against a record it
already keeps. The marginal cost is one transaction.

The opposite design choice exists and is worth naming. Zieglmeier et al.\ build
transparency over employee data on a permissionless chain precisely to avoid a
trusted arbiter, reasoning that in a workplace the party who would arbitrate is the
party with power over the worker~\cite{zieglmeier2023inverse}. We do the reverse and
make the employer the attester, because an attestation nobody authoritative stands
behind is what the animator already has. Which choice is right depends on whether the
asymmetry to be corrected is surveillance or invisibility, and here it is the latter.

The other objection is disclosure, and it does not dissolve. The fact that this
studio commissioned this person pre-exists the certificate, but its publication
does not, and publication is precisely what the credential is for. The metadata
schema can omit the studio name where a non-disclosure agreement applies
(\cref{subsec:privacy}), but an attestation whose attester is unnamed cannot be
weighed by a verifier, so the privacy lever and the value of the credential move
against each other. We therefore expect willingness to co-sign to vary with what a
studio believes its subcontracting depth would reveal, and we have no evidence
either way; obtaining it is the priority we identify in \cref{sec:conclusion}.

There is also a case that credentials of this kind make things worse. Wheelahan
and Moodie argue that micro-credentialing in casualised labour markets can
reproduce the dynamics of the waterfront shape-up, sorting an oversupplied
workforce more finely without improving anyone's
position~\cite{wheelahan2021gig}. The concern applies here. A record that makes
contribution legible also makes it comparable, and in a market with more animators
than work, finer comparison is not obviously in the animator's interest. Our
answer is narrow: what this framework certifies is that work was done and accepted,
not how well, so it adds an axis of verification rather than one of ranking. That
distinction holds only as long as nobody builds a score on top of it, which we
cannot prevent.

Regulatory pressure is a weaker inducement than it first appears, and
\cref{subsec:integration} gives the reason: the rules reaching this industry require a
detailed transaction record and say nothing about attribution. An attestation of who
did the work discharges no obligation under them. It is a tailwind, not a compliance
requirement.

One barrier is ours to manage rather than to observe. The design deliberately
hides the chain from the animator, and that abstraction must not extend to the
consequences: issuance creates a permanent public record, and while the commitment
of \cref{subsec:privacy} keeps the name out of it, the role, volume and period stay
legible by design. Onboarding has to disclose that plainly, because a credential the
holder did not understand they were publishing is worse than no credential.

% ----- 7.2 Trust Model and Abuse Prevention -----
\subsection{Trust Model and Abuse Prevention}
\label{subsec:trust}

The chain guarantees that a claim was recorded at a time by an issuer. It does not
guarantee that the work was done. Everything in this subsection follows from that
boundary.

\Cref{sec:problem} left a question here: a signal separates types only if it is
costly to send falsely, so does a studio countersignature qualify? Taken as a
quality signal it does not, and we should not claim otherwise. Signing truthfully
costs one transaction, so if the mechanism worked as intended every studio would
sign for every accepted delivery and the presence of a certificate would not
distinguish a good animator from an adequate one. What is costly to send falsely
is a countersignature for work that was never accepted, since it contradicts the
studio's own books and sits in public beside them. The separation the mechanism
achieves is therefore cruder than merit and, we think, the one that is missing:
between an animator who did the work and one who says so. That is closer to
Akerlof's remedy than to Spence's---a warranty that makes a claim checkable rather
than a cost only some can bear---and it is why we call this a verification device
and not a measure of quality.

This is also what the anchor is for, and it bounds the attacks that follow. The
obvious one is a fabricated log: an animator who controls their own entries can
mint a certificate for work they did not do, and hashing does not help, because
the hash fixes a claim rather than checking it. Issuing against an accepted order
means fabricating a commercial transaction instead, which the animator cannot do
alone. It does not close the attack---the acceptance is recorded in a system the
operator runs---and a co-signature is what puts a second party's key behind it.

Two attacks come from outside the design's assumptions rather than from within
them, and both are recent enough that we can only note them.

The first breaks the premise that an address corresponds to a person. Austgen et
al.\ show that trusted-execution techniques let a holder rent, share or pool a
single address without leaving a trace on chain, and name soulbound tokens among
the mechanisms this defeats~\cite{austgen2025liquefaction}. A credential bound to
an address inherits the problem: non-transferability of the token does not imply
non-transferability of control over the address holding it. The anchor bounds what
such an arrangement can manufacture, for the reason above, and does not restore the
assumption that one soul is one animator.

The second concerns what a holder chooses to show. Song et al.\ identify
cherry-picking as the structural weakness of holder-controlled credentials: a
portfolio that omits unfavourable entries is indistinguishable from a complete
one, and they enforce disclosure per issuer-domain to close it~\cite{song2026chaincred}.
Nothing here prevents an animator from presenting only the productions that went
well. That is not obviously a defect for a portfolio, which has never claimed
completeness, but it does bound what a verifier may infer from what they are
shown, and a verifier who treats a set of certificates as a career record rather
than a selection will over-read it.

Sybil resistance and open enrolment pull against each other, and we have not
resolved it. Tying wallet creation to registration in the management client gates
identity only as far as that registration is closed; completeness and minimal
friction both argue for letting any animator enrol freely. An external identity
protocol would settle it at the cost of the onboarding friction the design set out
to avoid. We state this as a trade-off we have chosen one side of, not as a solved
problem.

Two smaller vectors are handled by construction. A compromised issuer key can mint
arbitrary certificates, which the role separation limits: the admin revokes the
issuer role and the fraudulent tokens are revoked on chain, leaving the audit
trail intact. Statistically implausible claims---an animator reporting several
hundred cuts in a week---can be held for review before minting, which is a
heuristic rather than a guarantee.

\textbf{Wallet loss.}
A credential bound to an address is lost with the address, and this is the unsolved
problem that would destroy the property the design exists to create. Account
abstraction takes private-key handling away from the animator but relocates the
dependency onto the wallet provider rather than removing it. A contract could re-mint
a holder's certificates to a new address on multi-signature approval, and the
direction is not ours: Weyl et al.\ propose recovering a wallet through the social
relationships the tokens themselves encode~\cite{buterin2022decentralized}, with
later work taking up SBT-bearing wallets
specifically~\cite{goldston2023recovery}. What remains open is whether social recovery
works for a population that does not hold the overlapping memberships it assumes.

\textbf{Surveillance.}
A durable, granular record of what an animator completed is also an instrument for
monitoring them, and the asymmetry it would operate across is
documented~\cite{jftc2025anime}; the concern is the one raised about
platform-mediated gig work generally~\cite{sannon2022privacy}. Two things in the
design limit it and neither disposes of it. \texttt{task\_count} aggregates per
production rather than per day, so the record does not carry a work rate. And the
commitment of \cref{subsec:privacy} keeps the holder's name out of the published
metadata, so an observer must link an address to a person first---which role, volume
and period may accomplish unaided in a small industry. We should be exact about what
the animator does \emph{not} control: every certificate held at an address is
queryable by anyone, and holder control extends to what is presented to a verifier,
not to what exists. Zero-knowledge proofs over attributes would change that, letting
an animator prove a threshold---more than a hundred cuts across more than five
productions---without exposing the detail underneath. We have not built it. The record
that helps an animator in hiring can be turned against them in monitoring, and that is
a cost to be weighed rather than a residual.

% ----- 7.3 Legal and Regulatory Considerations -----
\subsection{Legal and Regulatory Considerations}
\label{subsec:legal}

The unresolved legal problem is data protection, and it is not incidental to the
design. Under Japan's Act on the Protection of Personal Information, a work history
linked to an identifiable individual is personal data. Pseudonymous wallet
addresses separate the record from the person only weakly, because project title, role
and period together may identify someone in a small industry. The commitment described
in \cref{subsec:privacy} keeps the name out of what is published and salts the digest,
which answers the objection that a hash of personal data is itself personal
data~\cite{finck2018blockchains}. What it does not answer is inference from the
attributes that have to stay legible for the certificate to be worth anything, so we do
not claim the published record is anonymous.

The Act also confers rights of correction and deletion that an immutable ledger
cannot honour: \texttt{revoke()} marks a record invalid, it does not remove it. We
should be careful not to present that as a law of nature. Ateniese et al.\ show
that a chameleon hash permits authorised redaction of a chain's
contents~\cite{ateniese2017redactable}, and the architecture we use---personal data
off chain, a hash and pointer on it---is itself a known response to this problem,
set out as such by Truong et al.~\cite{truong2020gdpr}. We adopt the second and not
the first, because redaction requires a trapdoor holder and we would be that
holder, which reintroduces at the protocol layer exactly the single-operator
dependency the design is trying to shed. The consequence is that our erasure story
remains the weaker one. Encrypting the off-chain metadata
and discarding the key would keep identifying data off the immutable layer, but we
have not implemented it. We treat metadata privacy as unresolved and would expect a
data protection impact assessment before any deployment with real personal data.

On classification we offer a reading, not advice: a non-transferable token with no
monetary value and no secondary market is unlikely to be a crypto-asset or a
security under the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act or the Payment Services
Act. That reading depends on the token carrying no entitlement, so it would not
survive an extension that attached one.

The framework is sometimes suggested as evidence in transactional disputes, given
the Fair Trade Commission's findings on unilateral pricing~\cite{jftc2025anime}.
It would evidence less than that suggests. A timestamped evidence hash establishes
that a claim existed at a time, not that the work was done or what was owed for it;
only a co-signature reaches the former, and nothing here reaches the latter.

% ----- 7.5 Generalizability -----
\subsection{Generalizability to Other Creative Industries}
\label{subsec:generalizability}

The problem this framework addresses is not particular to animation. It recurs
wherever work is organised in projects, subcontracted through two or three tiers,
credited under a cap, and produced by an organisation that may not outlive the
work. What varies across those settings is only whether an institution exists to
adjudicate attribution, and the evidence from settings where none does is
consistent.

Video game development has had a voluntary crediting standard since 2007. Its
current revision reports that 299 of 582 surveyed developers---51.3\%---never,
seldom or only sometimes receive official credit, and that 83.1\% were unsure of
or denied having any employer credit policy; the word ``enforce'' does not appear
in the document, and the remedy it offers an uncredited developer is to file a
bug~\cite{igda2023crediting}. A stratified study of 100 games found the omission
to be systematic rather than incidental~\cite{svelch2022credit}. Visual effects
supplies a sharper test, because it sits inside the same films as the crafts that
do have jurisdiction: on one 2023 release, 27 of more than 160 visual-effects
workers at a single vendor were credited, and the absence of a union was given as
the reason no remedy existed~\cite{amidi2023vfx}. Scholarly publishing has the
most widely adopted attribution standard of any field and states in the same
document that it has no authority to enforce it and that arbitrating authorship is
not the editor's role~\cite{icmje2026recommendations}.

The pattern is that a standard without an adjudicator does not produce accurate
attribution, and that building the adjudicator requires an institution with
authority over the parties---which is available in some industries and not in
others. A certification layer is a different response to the same problem: rather
than creating an authority, it makes an attestation that already occurred, at the
moment work was accepted and paid for, durable and portable beyond the party that
made it. Nothing in that argument is specific to animation. The same move has been
put to a quite different institutional act: Revolidis et al.\ propose a ledger for the
service of judicial documents across EU member states, valuing immutability for the
procedural reliability it gives the record of an act that must be provable
afterwards~\cite{revolidis2025justice}. Adapting the framework here to another setting
requires changing the role vocabulary and the unit that \texttt{task\_count} measures;
the contract, issuance service and verification portal do not change.

\textbf{International subcontracting.}
In-betweening is the stage most often sent abroad, to studios in South Korea, China and
the Philippines~\cite{mori2011pitfall,tschang2010outsourcing}, and a domestic shortage
of in-betweeners was reported in 2023 to be driving further overseas
orders~\cite{oki2026survey}. This widens the attribution gap rather than narrowing it,
because an overseas subcontractor sits further from the credit roll than a domestic one.
A wallet address is indifferent to the holder's country, so the mechanism itself does not
stop at a border. What does not travel is everything around it: the attester has to be a
party the overseas studio actually deals with, the data-protection analysis of
\cref{subsec:legal} has to be redone under whichever law reaches the holder, and the
metadata vocabulary is Japanese. We claim the mechanism is portable across
jurisdictions, and claim nothing about a deployment being so.
