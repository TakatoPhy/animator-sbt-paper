% Discussion

This section examines the practical challenges of deploying AnimatorSBT
in the anime industry (\cref{subsec:adoption_barriers}),
discusses the trust model and potential abuse vectors (\cref{subsec:trust}),
considers legal and regulatory implications (\cref{subsec:legal}),
explains why a technological solution is particularly needed
in Japan's anime industry (\cref{subsec:why_japan}),
and explores generalizability to other creative industries (\cref{subsec:generalizability}).

% ----- 7.1 Adoption Barriers -----
\subsection{Adoption Barriers}
\label{subsec:adoption_barriers}

The barriers to this framework are not technical, and the sharpest of them is
internal to the design. Co-signing is what converts a self-report into a
credential, and the transparency it creates is what a studio has reason to avoid:
a countersigned record of who did which cut also documents how deep the
outsourcing chain runs. The party whose participation the credential needs is
therefore the party it exposes. We can see no way to resolve this from the design
side, and we have no evidence about studio willingness either way; obtaining it is
the priority we identify in \cref{sec:conclusion}.

Regulatory pressure is a weaker inducement than it first appears. The Fair Trade
Commission's findings concern unilateral pricing and unpaid retakes, not
attribution~\cite{jftc2025anime}, and an attestation of who did the work does not
establish that it was fairly priced. It is a tailwind, not a compliance obligation
the framework discharges.

One barrier is ours to manage rather than to observe. The design deliberately
hides the chain from the animator, and that abstraction must not extend to the
consequences: issuance creates a permanent public record, in the present schema
with identifying fields in cleartext (\cref{subsec:privacy}). Onboarding has to
disclose that plainly, because a credential the holder did not understand they
were publishing is worse than no credential.

% ----- 7.2 Trust Model and Abuse Prevention -----
\subsection{Trust Model and Abuse Prevention}
\label{subsec:trust}

\textbf{It is important to acknowledge a fundamental limitation:}
AnimatorSBT's current trust model relies on self-reported work logs.
The blockchain guarantees that a \textit{claim was recorded at a specific time
by a specific issuer}, but it does not independently verify
that the claimed work was actually performed.
In other words, the SBT certifies the existence and immutability
of a contribution claim, not the truth of that claim.
This distinction is critical for understanding the system's
current trust boundary.

Without studio co-signing, AnimatorSBT-issued SBTs are
functionally \textit{self-attestations with cryptographic integrity}---
more reliable than verbal claims (due to timestamping, immutability,
and evidence hashing) but less authoritative than
mutually verified credentials.
This introduces potential abuse vectors that must be addressed:

\textbf{Fabricated contributions.}
An animator could create false work log entries in the PMC
to mint SBTs for work they did not perform.
We propose three mitigation layers:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Studio co-signing (recommended):}
    Extend the smart contract with a two-party confirmation mechanism
    where the studio (or production manager) countersigns the SBT.
    This transforms the SBT from a self-attestation into a mutually verified record.
  \item \textbf{Peer attestation:}
    Allow other animators who worked on the same production to attest
    to a colleague's contribution, creating a social verification layer.
  \item \textbf{Statistical anomaly detection:}
    The Issuance Service can flag statistically improbable claims
    (e.g., an animator claiming 500 cuts in a single week)
    for manual review before minting.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Sybil attacks.}
A malicious actor could create multiple wallets to fabricate a diverse portfolio.
Account abstraction mitigates this partially by linking wallet creation
to PMC registration, but a determined attacker could create multiple PMC accounts.
We acknowledge a tension here that we did not initially make explicit: tying wallet
creation to PMC registration as a Sybil gate is only as strong as PMC enrollment is
\emph{closed}, yet R1 (completeness) and R5 (minimal friction) call for \emph{open},
low-friction self-enrollment so that any animator can self-report. A meaningful
identity gate and frictionless open onboarding cannot both hold fully; resolving
this likely requires external identity protocols (e.g., Proof of Humanity,
Worldcoin), at the cost of some onboarding friction---a deliberate trade-off rather
than a solved problem.

\textbf{Issuer key compromise.}
If the Issuance Service's private key is compromised,
an attacker could mint arbitrary SBTs.
The AccessControl pattern mitigates this:
the admin role (held by TOONIQ) can revoke the compromised issuer role
and assign a new key, and all fraudulently minted SBTs can be revoked on-chain.

\textbf{Wallet loss and credential recovery.}
Because SBTs are bound to a specific wallet address,
loss of wallet access means loss of all accumulated credentials.
Account abstraction mitigates this by removing direct private key management,
but the underlying risk remains if the embedded wallet provider
experiences a service disruption.
A social recovery mechanism---where a set of designated guardians
(e.g., trusted colleagues, the PMC operator) can authorize wallet migration
to a new address---is a promising direction.
The smart contract could support a \texttt{migrate()} function
that re-mints all SBTs to a new address upon multi-signature approval,
preserving the credential history.
This is an open problem in the broader SBT literature~\cite{buterin2022decentralized},
with recent work exploring social recovery mechanisms
for SBT-bearing wallets~\cite{goldston2023recovery}.

\textbf{Surveillance and power asymmetry.}
A system that creates persistent, granular records of every task
an animator completes could be repurposed as a surveillance tool.
Studios or clients could use SBT data to monitor productivity
(e.g., ``this animator only completed 20 cuts last month''),
compare animators against each other, or penalize those deemed underperforming.
This risk is particularly acute given the existing power imbalance
between studios and freelance animators~\cite{jftc2025anime},
and mirrors broader concerns about surveillance
in platform-mediated gig work~\cite{sannon2022privacy}.
AnimatorSBT mitigates this through two design choices:
(1)~the animator controls which SBTs are publicly visible
via the selective disclosure mechanism (\cref{subsec:privacy}),
and (2)~metadata granularity can be adjusted---the \texttt{task\_count} field
reports aggregate totals per project, not daily output.
Beyond policy, a technical mitigation we consider promising---and pull forward from
future work (\cref{sec:conclusion})---is \emph{zero-knowledge aggregate proofs}:
an animator could prove a threshold property (e.g., ``$>$100 cuts across
$>$5 productions'') without revealing per-project or per-task detail, structurally
limiting the granularity available for surveillance. We present this as a
conditional, unimplemented mitigation, not a current feature.
Nevertheless, the tension between verifiability and privacy
is inherent to any credentialing system, and deployment must be accompanied
by clear policies that prevent misuse of contribution data
for punitive performance monitoring. We regard this surveillance risk as a
first-class concern, not a residual one: the same record that empowers an animator
in hiring can be turned against them in monitoring, and that cost must be weighed
against the benefit rather than assumed away.

% ----- 7.3 Legal and Regulatory Considerations -----
\subsection{Legal and Regulatory Considerations}
\label{subsec:legal}

The unresolved legal problem is data protection, and it is not incidental to the
design. Under Japan's Act on the Protection of Personal Information, a work history
linked to an identifiable individual is personal data. Pseudonymous wallet
addresses separate the record from the person only weakly---project title, role and
period together may identify someone in a small industry---and in the present
schema the separation does not arise at all, because identifying fields are stored
in cleartext on public IPFS (\cref{subsec:privacy}). The Act also confers rights of
correction and deletion that an immutable ledger cannot honour: \texttt{revoke()}
marks a record invalid, it does not remove it. Encrypting the off-chain metadata
and discarding the key would keep identifying data off the immutable layer, but we
have not implemented it. We treat metadata privacy as unresolved and would expect a
data protection impact assessment before any deployment with real personal data.

On classification we offer a reading, not advice: a non-transferable token with no
monetary value and no secondary market is unlikely to be a crypto-asset or a
security under the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act or the Payment Services
Act. That reading depends on the token carrying no entitlement, so it would not
survive an extension that attached one.

The framework is sometimes suggested as evidence in transactional disputes, given
the Fair Trade Commission's findings on unilateral pricing~\cite{jftc2025anime}.
It would evidence less than that suggests. A timestamped evidence hash establishes
that a claim existed at a time, not that the work was done or what was owed for it;
only a co-signature reaches the former, and nothing here reaches the latter.

\subsection{Why a Technological Solution Is Needed}
\label{subsec:why_japan}

A natural question is why the anime industry requires a blockchain-based solution
when other entertainment industries manage attribution through institutional means.
In Hollywood, labor unions such as the Screen Actors Guild--American Federation
of Television and Radio Artists (SAG-AFTRA),
the Writers Guild of America (WGA),
and the International Alliance of Theatrical Stage Employees (IATSE) enforce credit rights through collective bargaining
agreements. These unions maintain records of members' work history,
negotiate credit arbitration processes, and can call strikes
to enforce compliance---as demonstrated by the 2023 SAG-AFTRA and WGA strikes.

Japan's anime industry lacks comparable institutional infrastructure.
Although JAniCA exists as an industry association,
it functions primarily as a survey and advocacy body
rather than a collective bargaining agent with enforcement power.
The key structural differences are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Unionization rate:}
    The anime industry has no equivalent of SAG-AFTRA's mandatory membership.
    JAniCA membership is voluntary and does not confer negotiating rights.
  \item \textbf{Freelance prevalence:}
    At roughly half the workforce (47.3\% in 2023~\cite{janica2023survey},
    69.6\% in 2019~\cite{janica2019survey}), the freelance share in anime far exceeds
    the ratio in unionized Western entertainment industries.
  \item \textbf{Credit governance:}
    In Hollywood, unions define credit rules (e.g., the WGA's credit arbitration process).
    In anime, credit decisions rest with studios and production committees
    with no external oversight.
  \item \textbf{Multi-layer subcontracting:}
    While Hollywood outsources VFX,
    anime's gross-subcontracting (\textit{gurosu}) system
    routinely delegates entire episodes through two or three tiers of subcontractors,
    creating deeper attribution gaps.
\end{itemize}

In the absence of institutional solutions,
a technological approach that gives individual animators
tamper-evident, self-sovereign records becomes not merely useful
but, we argue, structurally valuable.
AnimatorSBT aims to provide part of what unions provide elsewhere:
a persistent, portable record of who did what---though, as discussed,
turning that record into a fully \emph{trusted} one requires the co-signing layer
of \cref{subsec:cosigning}.

More broadly, verifiable career records \emph{could} contribute to the social
capital of the animator profession---but we are careful not to overstate this.
The records this system produces attest to \emph{work performed}, not \emph{income},
and Japanese institutions such as banks and immigration authorities rely on income
documents (e.g., tax returns and withholding statements), not on-chain records,
which they do not currently recognize (\cref{subsec:legal}). We therefore do
\emph{not} claim that AnimatorSBT directly unlocks mortgages, visas, or credit.
The realistic, longer-term possibility is that a co-signed
(\cref{subsec:cosigning}), institutionally recognized attribution record could one
day \emph{complement} the income documentation these processes require---contingent
on co-signing, legal recognition, and integration with recognized institutional
rails (\cref{sec:conclusion}). We frame this as aspiration and future work, not a
demonstrated capability.

% ----- 7.5 Generalizability -----
\subsection{Generalizability to Other Creative Industries}
\label{subsec:generalizability}

The problem this framework addresses is not particular to animation. It recurs
wherever work is organised in projects, subcontracted through several layers,
credited under a cap, and produced by an organisation that may not outlive the
work. What varies across those settings is only whether an institution exists to
adjudicate attribution, and the evidence from settings where none does is
consistent.

Video game development has had a voluntary crediting standard since 2007. Its
current revision reports that 299 of 582 surveyed developers---51.3\%---never,
seldom or only sometimes receive official credit, and that 83.1\% were unsure of
or denied having any employer credit policy; the word ``enforce'' does not appear
in the document, and the remedy it offers an uncredited developer is to file a
bug~\cite{igda2023crediting}. A stratified study of 100 games found the omission
to be systematic rather than incidental~\cite{svelch2022credit}. Visual effects
supplies a sharper test, because it sits inside the same films as the crafts that
do have jurisdiction: on one 2023 release, 27 of more than 160 visual-effects
workers at a single vendor were credited, and the absence of a union was given as
the reason no remedy existed~\cite{amidi2023vfx}. Scholarly publishing has the
most widely adopted attribution standard of any field and states in the same
document that it has no authority to enforce it and that arbitrating authorship is
not the editor's role~\cite{icmje2026recommendations}.

The pattern is that a standard without an adjudicator does not produce accurate
attribution, and that building the adjudicator requires an institution with
authority over the parties---which is available in some industries and not in
others. A certification layer is a different response to the same problem: rather
than creating an authority, it makes an attestation that already occurred, at the
moment work was accepted and paid for, durable and portable beyond the party that
made it. Nothing in that argument is specific to animation. Adapting the framework
to another setting requires changing the role vocabulary and the unit that
\texttt{task\_count} measures; the contract, issuance service and verification
portal do not change.

\textbf{International subcontracting.}
Anime production increasingly involves cross-border outsourcing,
with key animation, in-betweening, and coloring tasks
frequently delegated to studios in South Korea, China, Vietnam,
and the Philippines~\cite{mori2011pitfall,tschang2010outsourcing}.
This international subcontracting deepens the attribution gap:
animators working overseas are even less likely to appear in Japanese credits
than domestic subcontractors.
AnimatorSBT is inherently borderless---blockchain credentials
are not jurisdiction-specific, and a wallet address works identically
regardless of the holder's country.
However, cross-border deployment introduces additional challenges:
local data protection regulations (e.g., China's Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL),
the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
for European co-productions), language barriers in metadata,
and the need for local PMC deployment or API integration
with existing production management tools used in each country.
Addressing these challenges is essential for AnimatorSBT
to cover the full scope of modern anime production.
