% ----- 7.1 Adoption Barriers -----
\subsection{Adoption Barriers}
\label{subsec:adoption_barriers}

The barriers to this framework are not technical, and the sharpest of them is
internal to the design. Co-signing is what converts a self-report into a
credential, and the transparency it creates is what a studio has reason to avoid:
a countersigned record of who did which cut also documents how deep the
outsourcing chain runs. The party whose participation the credential needs is
therefore the party it exposes.

Anchoring issuance on an accepted order separates two objections that are easily
conflated. One is effort: a studio asked to vouch for work must review it, decide and
stand behind the decision. That largely dissolves, because accepting a delivery and
paying for it \emph{is} the review, and it has already happened by the time a
certificate could be issued. The co-signature signs a determination the studio made for
its own commercial reasons against a record it already keeps, at a marginal cost of one
transaction.

The opposite design choice exists and is worth naming. Zieglmeier et al.\ build
transparency over employee data on a permissionless chain precisely to avoid a
trusted arbiter, reasoning that in a workplace the party who would arbitrate is the
party with power over the worker~\cite{zieglmeier2023inverse}. We do the reverse and
make the employer the attester, because an attestation nobody authoritative stands
behind is what the animator already has. Which choice is right depends on whether the
asymmetry to be corrected is surveillance or invisibility, and here it is the latter.

The other objection is disclosure, and it does not dissolve. That this studio
commissioned this person pre-exists the certificate; its publication does not, and
publication is what the credential is for. The schema can omit the studio name where a
non-disclosure agreement applies (\cref{subsec:privacy}), but a verifier cannot weigh an
attestation whose attester is unnamed, so the privacy lever and the value of the
credential move against each other. We expect willingness to co-sign to vary with what a
studio believes its subcontracting depth would reveal, and we have no evidence either
way; obtaining it is the priority of \cref{sec:conclusion}.

Credentials of this kind may also make things worse. Wheelahan and Moodie argue that
micro-credentialing in casualised labour markets reproduces the dynamics of the
waterfront shape-up, sorting an oversupplied workforce more finely without improving
anyone's position~\cite{wheelahan2021gig}. The concern applies here: a record that makes
contribution legible makes it comparable, and where there are more animators than work,
finer comparison is not obviously in the animator's interest. Our answer is narrow. What
this certifies is that work was done and accepted, not how well, so it adds an axis of
verification rather than one of ranking---a distinction that holds only as long as
nobody builds a score on top of it, which we cannot prevent.

Regulatory pressure is a weaker inducement than it first appears, and
\cref{subsec:integration} gives the reason: the rules reaching this industry require a
detailed transaction record and say nothing about attribution. An attestation of who
did the work discharges no obligation under them. It is a tailwind, not a compliance
requirement.

One barrier is ours to manage rather than observe. The design hides the chain from the
animator, and that abstraction must not extend to the consequences: issuance creates a
permanent public record, and while the commitment of \cref{subsec:privacy} keeps the name
out of it, role, volume and period stay legible by design. Onboarding has to say so
plainly, because a credential the holder did not understand they were publishing is worse
than no credential.

% ----- 7.2 Trust Model and Abuse Prevention -----
\subsection{Trust Model and Abuse Prevention}
\label{subsec:trust}

The chain guarantees that a claim was recorded at a time by an issuer, not that the
work was done. Everything here follows from that boundary.

\Cref{sec:problem} left a question: a signal separates types only if it is costly to
send falsely, so does a studio countersignature qualify? As a quality signal it does
not. Signing truthfully costs one transaction, so if the mechanism worked as intended
every studio would sign for every accepted delivery, and the presence of a certificate
would not distinguish a good animator from an adequate one. What is costly to send
falsely is a countersignature for work that was never accepted, since it contradicts the
studio's own books and sits in public beside them. The separation achieved is therefore
cruder than merit and, we think, the one that is missing: between an animator who did the
work and one who says so. That is closer to Akerlof's remedy than to Spence's---a
warranty making a claim checkable rather than a cost only some can bear---which is why we
call this a verification device and not a measure of quality.

This is what the anchor is for, and it bounds the attacks. The obvious one is a
fabricated log: an animator controlling their own entries can mint a certificate for
work they did not do, and hashing does not help, because the hash fixes a claim rather
than checking it. Issuing against an accepted order means fabricating a commercial
transaction instead, which the animator cannot do alone. It does not close the
attack---the acceptance is recorded in a system the operator runs---and a co-signature is
what puts a second party's key behind it.

Two attacks come from outside the design's assumptions, and both are recent enough that
we can only note them. The first breaks the premise that an address corresponds to a
person: Austgen et al.\ show that trusted-execution techniques let a holder rent, share
or pool a single address without leaving a trace on chain, and name soulbound tokens
among the mechanisms this defeats~\cite{austgen2025liquefaction}. Non-transferability of
the token does not imply non-transferability of control over the address holding it. The
anchor bounds what such an arrangement can manufacture and does not restore the
assumption that one soul is one animator.

The second concerns what a holder chooses to show. Song et al.\ identify cherry-picking
as the structural weakness of holder-controlled credentials, a portfolio omitting
unfavourable entries being indistinguishable from a complete one, and enforce disclosure
per issuer-domain to close it~\cite{song2026chaincred}. Nothing here prevents an animator
from presenting only the productions that went well. That is not obviously a defect for a
portfolio, which never claimed completeness, but it bounds what a verifier may infer, and
one who treats a set of certificates as a career record rather than a selection will
over-read it.

Sybil resistance and open enrolment pull against each other, unresolved. Tying wallet
creation to registration in the management client gates identity only as far as that
registration is closed, while completeness and minimal friction both argue for letting
any animator enrol freely. An external identity protocol would settle it at the cost of
the onboarding friction the design set out to avoid. This is a trade-off we have chosen
a side of, not a solved problem.

Two smaller vectors are handled by construction. A compromised issuer key can mint
arbitrary certificates, which the role separation limits: the admin revokes the
issuer role and the fraudulent tokens are revoked on chain, leaving the audit
trail intact. Statistically implausible claims---an animator reporting several
hundred cuts in a week---can be held for review before minting, which is a
heuristic rather than a guarantee.

\textbf{Wallet loss.}
A credential bound to an address is lost with the address, and this is the unsolved
problem that would destroy the property the design exists to create. Account abstraction
takes private-key handling away from the animator but relocates the dependency onto the
wallet provider rather than removing it. A contract could re-mint a holder's certificates
to a new address on multi-signature approval; the direction is not ours, Weyl et al.\
proposing recovery through the social relationships the tokens themselves
encode~\cite{buterin2022decentralized} and later work taking up SBT-bearing
wallets~\cite{goldston2023recovery}. What remains open is whether social recovery works
for a population that does not hold the overlapping memberships it assumes.

\textbf{Surveillance.}
A durable, granular record of what an animator completed is also an instrument for
monitoring them, across a documented asymmetry~\cite{jftc2025anime}, and the concern is
the one raised about platform-mediated gig work generally~\cite{sannon2022privacy}. Two
things limit it and neither disposes of it: \texttt{task\_count} aggregates per production
rather than per day, so the record carries no work rate, and the commitment of
\cref{subsec:privacy} keeps the name out of the published metadata, so an observer must
link an address to a person first---which role, volume and period may accomplish unaided
in a small industry. What the animator does \emph{not} control should be stated exactly:
every certificate held at an address is queryable by anyone, and holder control extends
to what is presented to a verifier, not to what exists. Zero-knowledge proofs over
attributes would change that, letting an animator prove a threshold---more than a hundred
cuts across more than five productions---without exposing the detail. We have not built
it. The record that helps an animator in hiring can be turned against them in monitoring,
and that is a cost to weigh rather than a residual.

% ----- 7.3 Legal and Regulatory Considerations -----
\subsection{Legal and Regulatory Considerations}
\label{subsec:legal}

The unresolved legal problem is data protection, and it is not incidental to the
design. Under Japan's Act on the Protection of Personal Information, a work history
linked to an identifiable individual is personal data. Pseudonymous wallet
addresses separate the record from the person only weakly, because project title, role
and period together may identify someone in a small industry. The commitment described
in \cref{subsec:privacy} keeps the name out of what is published and salts the digest,
which answers the objection that a hash of personal data is itself personal
data~\cite{finck2018blockchains}. What it does not answer is inference from the
attributes that have to stay legible for the certificate to be worth anything, so we do
not claim the published record is anonymous.

The Act also confers rights of correction and deletion that an immutable ledger cannot
honour: \texttt{revoke()} marks a record invalid rather than removing it. That is not a
law of nature. Ateniese et al.\ show that a chameleon hash permits authorised redaction
of a chain's contents~\cite{ateniese2017redactable}, and the architecture we use, personal
data off chain with a hash and pointer on it, is itself a known response set out as such
by Truong et al.~\cite{truong2020gdpr}. We adopt the second and not the first, because
redaction requires a trapdoor holder and we would be that holder, reintroducing at the
protocol layer the single-operator dependency the design is trying to shed. Our erasure
story is therefore the weaker one. Encrypting the off-chain metadata and discarding the
key would keep identifying data off the immutable layer; we have not implemented it, and
we would expect a data protection impact assessment before any deployment with real
personal data.

On classification we offer a reading, not advice: a non-transferable token with no
monetary value and no secondary market is unlikely to be a crypto-asset or a security
under the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act or the Payment Services Act. That
depends on the token carrying no entitlement, so it would not survive an extension that
attached one.

The framework is sometimes suggested as evidence in transactional disputes, given the
Fair Trade Commission's findings on unilateral pricing~\cite{jftc2025anime}. It would
evidence less than that suggests. A timestamped evidence hash establishes that a claim
existed at a time, not that the work was done or what was owed for it; only a
co-signature reaches the former, and nothing here reaches the latter.

% ----- 7.5 Generalizability -----
\subsection{Generalizability to Other Creative Industries}
\label{subsec:generalizability}

The problem is not particular to animation. It recurs wherever work is organised in
projects, subcontracted through two or three tiers, credited under a cap, and produced by
an organisation that may not outlive the work. What varies is only whether an institution
exists to adjudicate attribution, and the evidence from settings where none does is
consistent.

Video game development has had a voluntary crediting standard since 2007. Its current
revision reports that 299 of 582 surveyed developers, 51.3\%, never, seldom or only
sometimes receive official credit, and that 83.1\% were unsure of or denied having any
employer credit policy; the word ``enforce'' does not appear in it, and the remedy it
offers an uncredited developer is to file a bug~\cite{igda2023crediting}. A stratified
study of 100 games found the omission systematic rather than
incidental~\cite{svelch2022credit}. Visual effects is the sharper test, sitting inside the
same films as the crafts that do have jurisdiction: on one 2023 release, 27 of more than
160 visual-effects workers at a single vendor were credited, and the absence of a union
was given as the reason no remedy existed~\cite{amidi2023vfx}. Scholarly publishing has
the most widely adopted attribution standard of any field, and states in the same document
that it has no authority to enforce it and that arbitrating authorship is not the editor's
role~\cite{icmje2026recommendations}.

A standard without an adjudicator does not produce accurate attribution, and building
the adjudicator requires an institution with authority over the parties, which is
available in some industries and not others. A certification layer answers the same
problem differently: rather than creating an authority, it makes an attestation that
already occurred, at the moment work was accepted and paid for, durable and portable
beyond the party that made it. Nothing in that argument is specific to animation, and the
same move has been put to a quite different institutional act---Revolidis et al.\ propose
a ledger for the service of judicial documents across EU member states, valuing
immutability for the procedural reliability it gives the record of an act that must be
provable afterwards~\cite{revolidis2025justice}. Adapting the framework to another setting
requires changing the role vocabulary and the unit \texttt{task\_count} measures; the
contract, issuance service and verification portal do not change.

\textbf{International subcontracting.}
In-betweening is the stage most often sent abroad, to studios in South Korea, China and
the Philippines~\cite{mori2011pitfall,tschang2010outsourcing}, and a domestic shortage
of in-betweeners was reported in 2023 to be driving further overseas
orders~\cite{oki2026survey}. This widens the attribution gap rather than narrowing it,
because an overseas subcontractor sits further from the credit roll than a domestic one.
A wallet address is indifferent to the holder's country, so the mechanism itself does not
stop at a border. What does not travel is everything around it: the attester has to be a
party the overseas studio actually deals with, the data-protection analysis of
\cref{subsec:legal} has to be redone under whichever law reaches the holder, and the
metadata vocabulary is Japanese. We claim the mechanism is portable across
jurisdictions, and claim nothing about a deployment being so.
