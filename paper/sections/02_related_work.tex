% ----- 2.1 Anime Production Workflow -----
\subsection{Anime Production Workflow}
\label{subsec:anime_workflow}

Anime production follows a multi-stage pipeline that typically involves dozens of specialized roles
distributed across multiple studios~\cite{matsunaga2025labor, oki2026survey}.
A single 24-minute television episode may require on the order of 3{,}000 individual drawings
and the coordination of key animators (\textit{genga}), in-between animators (\textit{douga}),
color designers (\textit{shiage}), background artists, compositors, and directors,
among others.

The pipeline is broadly divided into three phases:
\textbf{pre-production} (planning, scriptwriting, storyboarding, character and background design),
\textbf{production} (layout, key animation, in-betweening, coloring, background painting),
and \textbf{post-production} (compositing, editing, sound design).
Each phase involves handoffs between specialized staff. Subcontracting operates at two
levels: studios specialising in a single phase take that phase's work from a prime
contractor~\cite{matsunaga2025labor}, and a studio may also take complete episodes,
a practice the industry names \textit{gurosu-uke}~\cite{oki2026survey}.

This structure creates the attribution problem this paper addresses, because the
credits follow the contract rather than the work. A trade-press guide to the ending
credits of a Japanese television episode reports that an episode produced by another
company is credited to that company as production assistance, and partially outsourced
work to the assisting companies by name, with individuals from them named only
sometimes~\cite{cirugeda2016credits}. The credit roll is therefore a curated subset of
who worked on an episode, and which individuals it resolves to depends on the level at
which the work was let.

% ----- 2.2 Freelance Animator Labor Conditions -----
\subsection{Freelance Animator Labor Conditions}
\label{subsec:labor}

Japan's anime production market reached a record \textyen 362.1 billion in 2024~\cite{tdb2025anime},
and the global anime market is projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate of 10.6\%,
reaching USD~49.6 billion by 2031~\cite{mordor2026anime}.
Despite this commercial success, the labor conditions of individual creators remain precarious.
Okeda and Koike~\cite{okeda2011animators} brought the findings of JAniCA's first
large-scale survey of animators' working conditions into the English-language
literature.

An exceptionally large share of the anime workforce works outside employment. In the
Japan Animation Creators Association's surveys, the two response categories that mean
this, \emph{furiiransu} and self-employed, together account for 69.6\% of respondents
in the 2019 wave, 47.3\% in 2023 and 37.0\% in
2026~\cite{janica2019survey,janica2023survey,janica2026survey}.\footnote{None of the
three is printed as a single number; each is the sum of the same two categories
(2019: 50.5\% freelance and 19.1\% self-employed of 382 respondents; 2023: 30.8\% and
16.5\% of 425; 2026: 26.0\% and 11.0\% of 970). Read the decline cautiously. The 2019
percentages are of all respondents and include a non-response bar, while the later two
exclude non-responses to the item; the 2026 report states that its conditions differed
from 2023's; and the share answering that a contract is unnecessary because they are
employed rises from 17.0\% to 43.8\% between the last two waves, which points to a
change in who responded and not only to a change in the industry.} Even the most recent
and lowest figure is roughly five times the share of the Japanese workforce as a whole
that is self-employed, 5.14~million of 67.13~million employed persons, or
7.7\%~\cite{mic2023labourforce}.\footnote{The comparison is indicative rather than
exact. The national figure counts self-employed proprietors and excludes unpaid family
workers, while JAniCA's is respondents' own description of their employment status.}
The Nippon Anime \& Film Culture Association (NAFCA) found in its 2024 survey that
the median monthly working hours for anime industry workers was 225 hours, with a
maximum of 336, and that 37.7\% of respondents reported monthly \emph{anime-related}
income below \textyen 200{,}000, approximately USD~1{,}300~\cite{nafca2024survey}.
The median hourly wage the same survey reports, \textyen 1{,}111, sits barely above
\textyen 1{,}004, the national weighted average minimum wage for
FY2023~\cite{mhlw2025minwage}.\footnote{The national weighted average has since risen
to \textyen 1{,}055 in FY2024 and \textyen 1{,}121 in FY2025~\cite{mhlw2025minwage},
so the margin over the statutory floor has closed rather than widened.}
The 225-hour median is about 38\% above the 163.0 hours a month worked on average by
general full-time employees across all industries in FY2023~\cite{mhlw2024monthly}.
The two are not measured the same way---the national series is reported by
establishments for employees, the NAFCA figure is self-reported and includes
freelancers---so the comparison indicates a gap without quantifying it precisely.

In-between animators (\textit{douga}) reported a mean annual income of
\textyen 2.632 million and key animators (\textit{genga}) \textyen 3.998 million in the
2023 wave, and \textyen 2.275 million and \textyen 4.152 million respectively in
2026~\cite{janica2023survey,janica2026survey}. Piece rates are what produce those
totals, and no body tabulates them. The figures that circulate come from respondents'
own free-text answers, which put an in-between drawing at \textyen 220 in the 2023 wave
and at \textyen 200 in
2026~\cite{janica2023survey,janica2026survey}.\footnote{Individual testimony rather
than a survey statistic. We report it because the per-drawing rate is the unit in which
in-betweening is commissioned, and no organisation publishes it.}

Regulation has moved quickly and in one direction. The Freelance Protection Act took
effect in November 2024, introducing protections for independent
contractors~\cite{meti2025anime}. In December 2025 Japan's Fair Trade Commission
published an investigation into the transaction environment for creators in anime
production, finding that unilateral price determination without negotiation may
constitute a violation of the statute governing payment to small and medium-sized
contractors~\cite{jftc2025anime}.\footnote{The survey covers film and anime together,
with a separate anime volume. The statute, Act No.~120 of 1956, was renamed with effect
from January 2026, and the report uses the current name.} In June 2026 the Cabinet
Office and the Commission issued guidelines for the industry as the policy response to
that investigation~\cite{jftc2026shishin}. None of it reaches the attribution problem:
these instruments regulate the terms on which work is commissioned, and
\cref{subsec:integration} shows that they require a detailed record of the transaction
while requiring nothing about who is recorded as having done the work.

% ----- 2.3 Soulbound Tokens and Decentralized Identity -----
\subsection{Soulbound Tokens and Decentralized Identity}
\label{subsec:sbt}

Weyl et al.~\cite{buterin2022decentralized} introduced the concept of \textbf{Soulbound Tokens (SBTs)}---non-transferable
tokens bound to a specific blockchain address (a ``Soul'')---as a primitive for encoding
commitments, credentials, and affiliations in a decentralized society.
Unlike conventional NFTs, SBTs cannot be sold or transferred,
making them suitable for representing earned credentials rather than speculative assets.
The original motivation drew from World of Warcraft's ``soulbound'' items,
which become permanently bound to a player upon acquisition~\cite{buterin2022soulbound}.

Since their introduction, SBTs have been explored in three adjacent domains.
Kakebayashi and Beverley~\cite{kakebayashi2023sbt} provided a comprehensive study of SBT implementations and
their potential as a social identity layer.
In healthcare, Pinna et al.\ report a case study issuing citizen-owned vaccination
certificates as SBTs, so that the holder rather than the issuing facility keeps the
credential~\cite{sbt_health2024}.
The authors of~\cite{sbt_credential_framework2023} developed a framework for privacy-preserving
credential issuance and verification using SBTs.
In agriculture, Verma et al.~\cite{verma2025sbt_agriculture} proposed a dual-blockchain
model using soulbound NFTs for farmer identity management.
The ERC-5192 standard~\cite{daubenschuetz2022erc5192} formalized a minimal interface
for soulbound NFTs on Ethereum, providing the technical foundation
for many of these implementations.

A parallel development is the W3C standard for \textbf{Verifiable Credentials (VCs)}~\cite{sporny2022vc}
and \textbf{Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs)}~\cite{sporny2022did}.
VCs are cryptographically signed digital credentials that can be verified without
contacting the original issuer,
following a three-party model of issuers, holders, and verifiers.
The authors of~\cite{did_survey2024} provide a comprehensive survey of DID and VC architectures.
Franklin et al.~\cite{franklin2025decentralized} implemented a decentralized certificate issuance and
verification system on Ethereum, while Berrios Moya et al.~\cite{berriosmoya2025zkp} proposed a zero-knowledge
proof-enabled system for academic records.

Beyond research prototypes, issuer-attested, holder-owned credentials have begun
shipping as products. Enterprise verifiable-credential platforms (e.g., Indicio,
Dock/Truvera, Credivera) and the Ethereum Attestation Service\footnote{\url{https://attest.org/}}
provide third-party-signed, recipient-owned attestations, and in Japan a
Dentsu\,/\,Sivira\,/\,Sony pilot issued non-transferable (soulbound) verifiable
credentials as portable achievement records.\footnote{\url{https://www.group.dentsu.com/jp/news/release/000680.html}}
We highlight this to be precise about our contribution: the credential
\emph{mechanism}---a third-party-attested, non-transferable, holder-owned record---is
a mature 2026 primitive, and we do not claim novelty in it. Our contribution is its
\emph{application} to anime production labor, described next.

\textbf{To the best of our knowledge---based on a search of
peer-reviewed venues, preprint servers, and industry whitepapers---no existing
work applies SBTs or VCs specifically to anime production workflows or to the
credentialing of freelance animators.}
The closest work is the Animechain.ai whitepaper~\cite{animechain2024whitepaper},
which proposes blockchain-based anime production management with AI integration,
but does not address individual-level contribution certification using SBTs
and has not been published as a peer-reviewed or preprint academic paper.

% ----- 2.4 Blockchain in Creative and Gig Economies -----
\subsection{Blockchain in Creative and Gig Economies}
\label{subsec:blockchain_creative}

The application of blockchain technology to the creative industries has been examined
from multiple perspectives.
Malik et al.\ survey blockchain in the creative industries, with direct
creator-to-consumer distribution among the opportunities they
identify~\cite{blockchain_creative2023}.
The World Economic Forum highlighted blockchain's potential for transparent
royalty distribution and intellectual property protection~\cite{wef2017creative}.

In the broader gig economy, blockchain has been discussed as a potential tool for
automated payment via smart contracts, reputation management,
and decentralized dispute resolution.
In principle, smart contracts can encode compensation terms that release funds
upon verified task completion, which has been proposed as a way to reduce the
payment delays that disproportionately affect freelancers; we note, however,
that such mechanisms presuppose a trustworthy oracle for ``verified completion''---
precisely the authenticity problem this paper foregrounds.

Credentialing a creative freelancer is where these primitives meet the present
setting. A portfolio shows finished work but not the holder's role in it, and nobody
vouches for the claim; the Open Badges specification, aligned to the same
verifiable-credentials data model, answers that by carrying the criteria a credential
was awarded against together with links to the evidence~\cite{openbadges3}.
Peric\`as-Gornals et al.\ add a property that matters wherever a credential can expose
its holder: in their scheme a recipient can reject a soulbound token, and the protocol
provides non-repudiation of its reception, so issuance is not
unilateral~\cite{sbt_credentials_digital2024}.

Where blockchain has reached anime specifically, it has reached the rights rather
than the work. The Four Pillars group identifies IP tokenization, compensation
transparency and fan engagement as the opportunities~\cite{fourpillars2025anime}, and
the ventures follow that line: Animechain/AIRA, ANIM.JP and ANIFTY operate on IP
rights, fan tokens, collectibles or AI-training-data provenance, and none issues a
worker-owned, task-level credential. The anime-and-web3 space and the
worker-credential space remain disjoint.

% ----- 2.5 Research Gap -----
\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{subsec:gap}

Credit attribution is not an unsolved problem everywhere. Film and television
solved it institutionally: under the Writers Guild of America's Basic Agreement,
credit for screen authorship may be given \emph{only} through the Guild's
procedure, is the one term a writer may not bargain individually, and the
employer is contractually bound to accept the arbitration committee's
determination~\cite{wga2023mba}. Fisk~\cite{fisk2010jurisdiction} argues that the
operative variable is jurisdiction---the Guild's exclusive authority to define
individual authorship of a collective work---and traces how that authority became
the Guild's central function~\cite{fisk2012screencredit}. The same form reaches
animation in the United States, where The Animation Guild's agreement requires
screen credit for the animation, story, background and layout classifications and
names a final adjudicator for disputes~\cite{tag2024cba}.

That route is unavailable in Japanese animation. It requires three things at once:
exclusive craft jurisdiction, a collective agreement binding employers to the
determination, and a standing arbitration apparatus. What removed the first of them
was the move to freelance contracting: a craft cannot hold exclusive jurisdiction over
work commissioned individually from people who are not its employees. Matsunaga's studio
ethnography shows what replaced it, which is not collective bargaining but individual
and studio-mediated coping---veteran animators securing short-term work against the
risk of an income gap, younger ones relying on the studio to find them work, and
freelancers thereby subject to a form of organisational control without the protections
of employment~\cite{matsunaga2023portfolio}.\footnote{Nagata and Matsunaga give the
book-length treatment of the same transition~\cite{nagata2022sociology}.} The bodies representing Japanese
animators today are accordingly general incorporated associations rather than unions
with bargaining jurisdiction, which is why the industry's own survey can document the
grievance (\cref{subsec:credit_gap}) without any body being in a position to
adjudicate it. Japanese copyright law offers no substitute~\cite{jp_copyright_act}: Article~16 confines
authorship of a cinematographic work to those who contribute to its
\emph{overall} shaping, which excludes artists working cut by cut, and
Article~19(3) permits the author's name to be omitted where consistent with fair
practice. The government's own model contract for freelance animators has the
animator agree not to exercise moral rights, and makes crediting the
commissioner's election~\cite{bunka2024model}.

operate in this setting, classified by where the record comes
from~\cite{oki2026survey}. The distinctions that matter are who vouches for an
entry, whether the record outlives the organisation that made it, and whether it
reaches work that never shipped.

Putting work records on a chain is not a new proposal, and we should say whose it
was. Sharples and Domingue set out a distributed store of educational and
professional achievement with a reputation layer above it a decade
ago~\cite{sharples2016kudos}, and EduCTX built a prototype carrying the idea into
academic credit, with an invitation to other institutions to
join~\cite{turkanovic2018eductx}. EduCTX assumes what this setting lacks. An
accredited university issuing its own credential is an institution with standing to
attest, and the design problem there is interoperability between such institutions
rather than the absence of any. What
changes here is not the ledger but where the attestation can originate when no
accredited issuer exists, which is why the anchor rather than the token is what
this paper argues about.

Nor does the alternative require a ledger at all. Open Badges issues portable,
verifiable, third-party-signed credentials at scale, aligned to the same
verifiable-credentials data model, with no chain
underneath~\cite{openbadges3}. Where an issuer is durable and identifiable, that
is the lighter answer, and the honest form of our claim is that it is the right
answer wherever the issuer outlives the credential.

Two responses outside this table deserve naming, because a reader may reasonably
prefer either. Scholarly publishing solved granular per-contributor attribution
without a ledger: a controlled taxonomy of roles, carried as metadata by a central
registry, now widely adopted~\cite{hosseini2026credit}. It works because
publishers already sit between authors and the record and can require the metadata
as a condition of publication---an intermediary position no party occupies in
animation, where the studio that holds the record is also the party the record
would expose. And the European Union has legislated a non-blockchain answer to the
same problem: professional qualifications are among the attributes a European
Digital Identity Wallet must carry, with provision due at the end of
2026~\cite{eu2024eudi}, and platform workers acquire a statutory right to the
portability of data generated through their work, ratings and reviews
included~\cite{eu2024platformwork}. That framework locates trust in the issuer's
legal status rather than in a ledger, and it presumes an issuer with standing to
attest---which returns the question this paper starts from.

No mechanism above the rule in the table covers all three columns, and the reason is
structural rather than technical. Institutional determination is authoritative
precisely because it is scarce: the Guild caps credited writers at two, three
only through arbitration, which is a policy of concentrating attribution rather
than recording it. Records derived from credits inherit whatever the credits
omit, and an unreleased production has no staff roll at all. Self-reported
records reach every task but carry no attestation. The gap is therefore not an absence of data. Production generates a per-cut,
per-person trail as a byproduct of getting the work done, and every prior mechanism
in the table either fails to reach that trail or reaches it without anyone standing
behind what it says.
