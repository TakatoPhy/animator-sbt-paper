% ----- 2.1 Anime Production Workflow -----
\subsection{Anime Production Workflow}
\label{subsec:anime_workflow}

Anime production follows a multi-stage pipeline that typically involves dozens of specialized roles
distributed across multiple studios~\cite{condry2013soul, animechain2024whitepaper}.
A single 24-minute television episode may require on the order of 3{,}000 individual drawings
and the coordination of key animators (\textit{genga}), in-between animators (\textit{douga}),
color designers (\textit{shiage}), background artists, compositors, and directors,
among others.

The pipeline is broadly divided into three phases:
\textbf{pre-production} (planning, scriptwriting, storyboarding, character and background design),
\textbf{production} (layout, key animation, in-betweening, coloring, background painting),
and \textbf{post-production} (compositing, editing, sound design).
Each phase involves handoffs between specialized staff, and it is common practice for studios to
\textbf{partially or fully outsource} individual episodes or specific tasks to subcontractors~\cite{matsunaga2025labor}.

This multi-layered subcontracting structure creates a critical attribution problem.
When an episode is partially outsourced, individual animators may be credited alongside in-house staff.
However, when entire episodes are \textit{gross}-subcontracted (\textit{gurosu}),
only the subcontracting studio---not the individual animators---may appear in the credits.
In extreme cases, secondary and tertiary subcontractors remain entirely uncredited,
rendering their contributions invisible to the broader industry.

% ----- 2.2 Freelance Animator Labor Conditions -----
\subsection{Freelance Animator Labor Conditions}
\label{subsec:labor}

Japan's anime production market reached a record \textyen 362.1 billion in 2024~\cite{tdb2025anime},
and the global anime market is projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate of 10.6\%,
reaching USD~49.6 billion by 2031~\cite{mordor2026anime}.
Despite this commercial success, the labor conditions of individual creators remain precarious.
Okeda and Koike~\cite{okeda2011animators} provided the first systematic assessment
of animators' working conditions based on JAniCA survey data.

An exceptionally large share of the anime workforce consists of freelancers.
The Japan Animation Creators Association (JAniCA) reported that 47.3\% of animation creators
were self-employed or freelance in 2023~\cite{janica2023survey}, down from 69.6\% in
2019~\cite{janica2019survey} but still approximately six times the 7.6\% share of
self-employed workers in the Japanese workforce as a whole~\cite{mic2023labourforce}.
The Nippon Anime \& Film Culture Association (NAFCA) found in its 2024 survey that
the median monthly working hours for anime industry workers was 225 hours (maximum: 336 hours),
compared to the national average of 162.3 hours~\cite{nafca2024survey}.
A further 37.7\% of respondents reported monthly \emph{anime-related} income
below \textyen 200{,}000 (approximately USD~1{,}300),
and the median hourly wage was \textyen 1{,}111---barely above
the national minimum wage average of \textyen 1{,}004
in effect at the time of the survey (FY2023)~\cite{nafca2024survey}.\footnote{The
national minimum wage was raised to \textyen 1{,}055 in October 2024 (FY2024).}

In-between animators (\textit{douga}) earned an average annual income of
\textyen 2.632 million, while key animators (\textit{genga}) earned \textyen 3.998 million~\cite{janica2023survey}.
These figures are particularly striking given that a single in-between frame
is typically compensated at approximately \textyen 200 (less than USD~2),
and each frame can require over an hour of work.\footnote{The per-frame rate and
time estimate are drawn from~\cite{ristola2017blood}, a 2017 graduate symposium
paper rather than a survey instrument, and are cited here illustratively; the income and wage figures
above are from the official JAniCA and NAFCA surveys.}

In December 2025, Japan's Fair Trade Commission published an investigation into
the transaction environment for creators in the anime production industry,
finding that unilateral price determination without negotiation may constitute a legal violation
under the Subcontract Act~\cite{jftc2025anime}.\footnote{The survey covers film and
anime together, with a separate anime volume. The Act was renamed with effect from
January 2026; the JFTC report already uses the new name.}
The Freelance Protection Act, which took effect in November 2024,
introduced new protections for independent contractors,
but its impact on the anime industry remains to be seen~\cite{meti2025anime}.

% ----- 2.3 Soulbound Tokens and Decentralized Identity -----
\subsection{Soulbound Tokens and Decentralized Identity}
\label{subsec:sbt}

Weyl et al.~\cite{buterin2022decentralized} introduced the concept of \textbf{Soulbound Tokens (SBTs)}---non-transferable
tokens bound to a specific blockchain address (a ``Soul'')---as a primitive for encoding
commitments, credentials, and affiliations in a decentralized society.
Unlike conventional NFTs, SBTs cannot be sold or transferred,
making them suitable for representing earned credentials rather than speculative assets.
The original motivation drew from World of Warcraft's ``soulbound'' items,
which become permanently bound to a player upon acquisition~\cite{buterin2022soulbound}.

Since their introduction, SBTs have been explored in three adjacent domains.
Kakebayashi and Beverley~\cite{kakebayashi2023sbt} provided a comprehensive study of SBT implementations and
their potential as a social identity layer.
In healthcare, SBTs have been proposed for privacy-aware medical record authentication,
enabling interoperable patient data sharing across facilities
while maintaining decentralized control~\cite{sbt_health2024}.
The authors of~\cite{sbt_credential_framework2023} developed a framework for privacy-preserving
credential issuance and verification using SBTs.
In agriculture, Verma et al.~\cite{verma2025sbt_agriculture} proposed a dual-blockchain model
integrating soulbound NFTs for farmer identity management and asset validation
across supply chains.
The ERC-5192 standard~\cite{daubenschuetz2022erc5192} formalized a minimal interface
for soulbound NFTs on Ethereum, providing the technical foundation
for many of these implementations.

A parallel development is the W3C standard for \textbf{Verifiable Credentials (VCs)}~\cite{sporny2022vc}
and \textbf{Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs)}~\cite{sporny2022did}.
VCs are cryptographically signed digital credentials that can be verified without
contacting the original issuer,
following a three-party model of issuers, holders, and verifiers.
The authors of~\cite{did_survey2024} provide a comprehensive survey of DID and VC architectures.
Franklin et al.~\cite{franklin2025decentralized} implemented a decentralized certificate issuance and
verification system on Ethereum, while Berrios Moya et al.~\cite{berriosmoya2025zkp} proposed a zero-knowledge
proof-enabled system for academic records.

Beyond research prototypes, issuer-attested, holder-owned credentials have begun
shipping as products. Enterprise verifiable-credential platforms (e.g., Indicio,
Dock/Truvera, Credivera) and the Ethereum Attestation Service\footnote{\url{https://attest.org/}}
provide third-party-signed, recipient-owned attestations, and in Japan a
Dentsu\,/\,Sivira\,/\,Sony pilot issued non-transferable (soulbound) verifiable
credentials as portable achievement records.\footnote{\url{https://www.group.dentsu.com/jp/news/release/000680.html}}
We highlight this to be precise about our contribution: the credential
\emph{mechanism}---a third-party-attested, non-transferable, holder-owned record---is
a mature 2026 primitive, and we do not claim novelty in it. Our contribution is its
\emph{application} to anime production labor, described next.

\textbf{To the best of our knowledge---based on a search of
peer-reviewed venues, preprint servers, and industry whitepapers---no existing
work applies SBTs or VCs specifically to anime production workflows or to the
credentialing of freelance animators.}
The closest work is the Animechain.ai whitepaper~\cite{animechain2024whitepaper},
which proposes blockchain-based anime production management with AI integration,
but does not address individual-level contribution certification using SBTs
and has not been published as a peer-reviewed or preprint academic paper.

% ----- 2.4 Blockchain in Creative and Gig Economies -----
\subsection{Blockchain in Creative and Gig Economies}
\label{subsec:blockchain_creative}

The application of blockchain technology to the creative industries has been examined
from multiple perspectives.
The authors of~\cite{blockchain_creative2023} surveyed the state of blockchain in creative industries,
identifying opportunities in rights management, provenance tracking, and direct
creator-to-consumer distribution.
The World Economic Forum highlighted blockchain's potential for transparent
royalty distribution and intellectual property protection~\cite{wef2017creative}.

In the broader gig economy, blockchain has been discussed as a potential tool for
automated payment via smart contracts, reputation management,
and decentralized dispute resolution.
In principle, smart contracts can encode compensation terms that release funds
upon verified task completion, which has been proposed as a way to reduce the
payment delays that disproportionately affect freelancers; we note, however,
that such mechanisms presuppose a trustworthy oracle for ``verified completion''---
precisely the authenticity problem this paper foregrounds.

The concept of \textbf{Verified Creator Portfolios (VCPs)} has emerged as
a direct application of blockchain to creative freelancer credentialing.
Unlike traditional portfolios, which show completed work but cannot prove
the creator's specific role, constraints, or the authenticity of their contribution,
blockchain-based credentials can carry verifiable evidence links,
criteria, and a verification method~\cite{sbt_credentials_digital2024}.

The Four Pillars research group argued specifically that ``Anime Needs Web3,''
identifying IP tokenization, creator compensation transparency,
and fan engagement as key opportunity areas~\cite{fourpillars2025anime}.
However, their analysis focused on IP-level tokenization (e.g., fractional ownership
of anime franchises) rather than individual creator-level contribution certification.
This pattern holds across current anime-and-blockchain ventures: initiatives such as
Animechain/AIRA,\footnote{\url{https://whitepaper.animechain.ai/}} ANIM.JP, and
ANIFTY operate at the level of IP rights, fan tokens, collectibles, or
AI-training-data provenance---none issues a worker-owned, task-level contribution
credential. The anime-and-web3 space and the worker-credential space remain disjoint.

% ----- 2.5 Research Gap -----
\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{subsec:gap}

Credit attribution is not an unsolved problem everywhere. Film and television
solved it institutionally: under the Writers Guild of America's Basic Agreement,
credit for screen authorship may be given \emph{only} through the Guild's
procedure, is the one term a writer may not bargain individually, and the
employer is contractually bound to accept the arbitration committee's
determination~\cite{wga2023mba}. Fisk~\cite{fisk2010jurisdiction} argues that the
operative variable is jurisdiction---the Guild's exclusive authority to define
individual authorship of a collective work---and traces how that authority became
the Guild's central function~\cite{fisk2012screencredit}. The same form reaches
animation in the United States, where The Animation Guild's agreement requires
screen credit for the animation, background and layout classifications and names
a final adjudicator for disputes~\cite{tag2024cba}.

That route is unavailable in Japanese animation, and not for want of trying. The
route requires three things at once: exclusive craft jurisdiction, a collective
agreement binding employers to the determination, and a standing arbitration
apparatus. The bodies representing Japanese animators are general incorporated
associations rather than unions with bargaining jurisdiction; performers in the
same productions, organised as a cooperative, do hold collective agreements,
which isolates the missing variable as organisational form rather than law or
custom. Japanese copyright law offers no substitute~\cite{jp_copyright_act}: Article~16 confines
authorship of a cinematographic work to those who contribute to its
\emph{overall} shaping, which excludes artists working cut by cut, and
Article~19(3) permits the author's name to be omitted where consistent with fair
practice. The government's own model contract for freelance animators has the
animator agree not to exercise moral rights, and makes crediting the
commissioner's election~\cite{bunka2024model}.

No mechanism in the table covers all three columns, and the reason is
structural rather than technical. Institutional determination is authoritative
precisely because it is scarce: the Guild caps credited writers at two, three
only through arbitration, which is a policy of concentrating attribution rather
than recording it. Records derived from credits inherit whatever the credits
omit, and an unreleased production has no staff roll at all. Self-reported
records reach every task but carry no attestation. What is missing is not a
record---production already generates a per-cut, per-person paper trail as a
byproduct---but a mechanism that makes that trail durable, portable and
contestable outside the organisation that holds it. That is the gap this paper
addresses, and to the best of our knowledge no prior work applies
non-transferable tokens to it.
