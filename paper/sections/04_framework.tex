% Proposed Framework: AnimatorSBT

We propose \textbf{AnimatorSBT}, a framework designed to integrate Soulbound Token issuance
into the anime production workflow through an existing production management tool.
This section describes the system architecture (\cref{subsec:architecture}),
the SBT metadata schema (\cref{subsec:metadata}),
the issuance flow (\cref{subsec:flow}),
the verification mechanism (\cref{subsec:verification}),
and privacy considerations (\cref{subsec:privacy}).

% ----- 4.1 System Architecture -----
\subsection{System Architecture}
\label{subsec:architecture}

AnimatorSBT consists of four components:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Production Management Client (PMC):}
    A Progressive Web Application (PWA) used by freelance animators to track
    their daily work---cut assignments, task status, deadlines, and deliverables.
    This is the primary data source for contribution records.
  \item \textbf{AnimatorSBT Smart Contract:}
    An ERC-721--based~\cite{entriken2018erc721} non-transferable token contract deployed on a Layer~2 blockchain
    (Polygon Proof of Stake (PoS)). The contract enforces soulbinding by overriding transfer functions
    and provides minting, revocation, and metadata retrieval interfaces.
  \item \textbf{Issuance Service:}
    A lightweight backend that bridges the PMC and the smart contract.
    Upon project completion (or at a user-defined milestone),
    the service constructs SBT metadata from the PMC's work logs,
    uploads it to the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS)~\cite{benet2014ipfs},
    and triggers the minting transaction.
  \item \textbf{Verification Portal:}
    A public web interface where third parties (studios, clients, recruiters)
    can verify an animator's contribution history by querying the blockchain
    and rendering the associated metadata.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{fig:architecture} illustrates the data flow between these components.

  \caption{AnimatorSBT system architecture. The animator interacts only with the

\textbf{Why blockchain rather than a centralized database?}
A conventional approach would store contribution records in
a centralized database operated by a single entity (e.g., TOONIQ or a studio).
However, this fails to satisfy the requirements defined in \cref{subsec:formalization}
for three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Persistence beyond any single organization:}
    Anime studios frequently close or restructure.
    A centralized database disappears with its operator.
    The on-chain record---token ownership, issuance timestamp, and the metadata
    URI---persists independently of any company's survival; even if TOONIQ ceases
    operations, these facts remain on the public blockchain.
    We flag one caveat here and quantify it in \cref{subsec:requirements_eval}:
    the \emph{human-readable} metadata lives off-chain on IPFS and depends on
    continued pinning, and the verification portal and issuance service are
    operated by TOONIQ. What strictly survives issuer death is therefore
    proof of existence and ownership, not the full human-readable credential
    unless the IPFS data is preserved by another party.
  \item \textbf{Cross-organizational portability:}
    Freelance animators work across dozens of studios over their careers.
    In a centralized model, each studio holds a fragment of an animator's history
    in its own siloed system, with no standard way to aggregate them.
    With SBTs, all credentials accumulate in a single wallet address.
    An animator can present one link that aggregates their PMC-tracked
    contributions across studios (subject to the authenticity caveat of
    \cref{subsec:cosigning} and the completeness caveat of
    \cref{subsec:requirements_eval}).
  \item \textbf{Tamper resistance without trust:}
    A centralized database administrator can modify or delete records.
    Blockchain records are append-only and cryptographically linked;
    once an SBT is minted, neither the issuer nor any third party
    can alter the fact that it was issued, when, and to whom.
    This provides animators with an independent guarantee
    that their credentials cannot be retroactively revoked or falsified
    without an auditable on-chain trace.
    (Issuance itself is \emph{permissioned}---only holders of the
    \texttt{ISSUER\_ROLE} may mint---so the system is not trustless at the point
    of issuance; \cref{subsec:trust} discusses the resulting trust assumptions.)
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why SBTs rather than Verifiable Credentials?}
An alternative design would use W3C Verifiable Credentials (VCs)~\cite{sporny2022vc}
instead of on-chain SBTs.
VCs offer mature standards, selective disclosure, and off-chain storage,
making them well suited for scenarios where a trusted issuer exists
and credentials are presented bilaterally (e.g., university diplomas).
We do not claim that VCs are unworkable here---a VC could itself be anchored
on-chain (e.g., by recording a credential hash or status) to mitigate
issuer-availability concerns~\cite{kakebayashi2023sbt}. Rather, our reasons for
choosing on-chain SBTs are ergonomic and pertain to requirements R4 (portability)
and R5 (minimal friction): SBTs provide a \emph{naturally aggregatable} portfolio---all
credentials accumulate at a single address and are queryable in one call---whereas
the standard VC model requires the holder to store, manage, and selectively present
individual credentials from a personal wallet, and verification typically routes
through the issuer's published key or a status registry that the issuer maintains.
For a low-friction, holder-passive experience aimed at non-technical animators,
the address-as-portfolio model is a better fit. We stress that SBTs and VCs are
\emph{not} mutually exclusive: future work (\cref{sec:conclusion}) explores
VC-compliant wrappers for AnimatorSBT metadata, which would add selective
disclosure and interoperability that on-chain SBTs lack.

\textbf{Why a bespoke contract rather than a general attestation service or a
signed log?}
Two lighter-weight alternatives deserve explicit treatment, as a reviewer might
reasonably prefer them.
First, a generic on-chain attestation registry such as the Ethereum Attestation
Service (EAS) could carry the same attestations without a custom contract.
We regard EAS as a genuinely viable substrate and do not claim our contract is the
only option; indeed the co-signing design of \cref{subsec:cosigning} could be
expressed as EAS attestations. We chose a minimal dedicated ERC-721 contract for
three pragmatic reasons: (i)~it bundles soulbound semantics, role-gated issuance,
and role-gated co-signing into a single auditable unit with no dependency on an
external registry's schema, governance, or continued operation; (ii)~it yields the
\emph{token-balance-as-portfolio} ergonomic (R4/R5) that a non-transferable token
gives natively and a raw attestation does not; and (iii)~it lets SBT-aware wallets
recognize the credential directly (via ERC-5192, \cref{subsec:contract}). These are
ergonomic and self-containment arguments, not claims of technical superiority.
Second, one could forgo blockchain entirely and publish a centrally operated,
cryptographically signed append-only log (e.g., a Certificate-Transparency--style
Merkle log with periodic public anchoring). Such a design achieves tamper-evidence
and is cheaper, but \emph{verification still depends on the operator continuing to
publish and serve the log and its signing key}; a public-chain record is verifiable
by anyone via any RPC node with no dependency on the issuer's survival, which is
precisely the property R3 (persistence beyond issuer death) and R4 (portability)
demand. We therefore view the signed-log approach as a reasonable lighter option for
integrity alone, but not for operator-independent persistence and portability.

A key design principle is \textbf{transparency to the end user}:
the animator should not need to understand blockchain technology,
manage a wallet manually, or pay gas fees.
In the production design, the PMC handles wallet creation
(embedded wallet via account abstraction~\cite{buterin2021erc4337})
and the Issuance Service sponsors gas fees through a relayer (ERC-2771),
so that from the animator's perspective the intended experience is:
``I finished my cuts $\rightarrow$ I got a certificate in my profile.''
We emphasize that this walletless, gasless experience is a \emph{design target}:
the current prototype uses a standard externally owned account and pays gas directly
(\cref{subsec:stack}), and the embedded-wallet/relayer integration---together with
its own failure modes such as wallet recovery (\cref{subsec:trust})---is not yet
implemented or evaluated with users.

% ----- 4.2 SBT Metadata Schema -----
\subsection{SBT Metadata Schema}
\label{subsec:metadata}

Each AnimatorSBT encodes a structured record of a single project contribution.
The metadata follows a JSON schema stored on IPFS, with the content hash
referenced by the on-chain token URI. \Cref{tab:metadata} defines the schema.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{AnimatorSBT metadata schema. The JSON follows an NFT-compatible
structure with top-level \texttt{name} and \texttt{description} fields;
contribution data is stored under an \texttt{attributes} object.}
\label{tab:metadata}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{3.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Description} \\
\midrule
\texttt{name}              & string   & Display name for the SBT \\
\texttt{description}       & string   & Human-readable summary \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{3}{@{}l}{\textit{attributes} (contribution data):} \\
\texttt{animator\_name}    & string   & Animator's display name \\
\texttt{animator\_wallet}  & address  & Animator's wallet address \\
\texttt{project}           & string   & Anime project or episode title \\
\texttt{role}              & enum     & e.g., \texttt{key\_anim}, \texttt{in\_between}, \texttt{coloring} \\
\texttt{task\_count}       & integer  & Tasks completed (cuts, frames) \\
\texttt{start\_date}       & date     & Contribution start date \\
\texttt{end\_date}         & date     & Contribution end date \\
\texttt{studio\_name}      & string   & Contracting studio (nullable) \\
\texttt{issued\_at}        & datetime & ISO~8601 issuance timestamp \\
\midrule
\texttt{evidence\_files}   & array    & IPFS URIs of supporting files \\
\texttt{evidence\_hash}    & bytes32  & Keccak-256 of serialized attributes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The \texttt{evidence\_hash} field provides a cryptographic link between the on-chain certificate
and the off-chain work log stored in the PMC.
This allows verifiers to confirm that the SBT corresponds to actual recorded work
without exposing the raw work log data.

The \texttt{studio\_name} field is intentionally nullable to accommodate cases where
non-disclosure agreements prevent public attribution to a specific project or studio.
Even without a studio name, the SBT still records the animator's self-declared role,
task volume, and time period, providing more information than the current system of
no record at all.
There is, however, a direct tension between this privacy affordance and authenticity
(\cref{subsec:cosigning}): when \texttt{studio\_name} is null there is no named
counterparty who could co-sign or be contacted, so a null-studio SBT cannot be
elevated above a self-attestation. The privacy feature is thus most needed
precisely for confidential, high-value projects---exactly the cases where
third-party verification is weakest. We make this trade-off explicit rather than
implying that every SBT can attain the same level of trust.

% ----- 4.3 Issuance Flow -----
\subsection{Issuance Flow}
\label{subsec:flow}

The SBT issuance process follows five steps,
illustrated in \Cref{fig:issuance_flow}:

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{figures/issuance_flow}
  \caption{Five-step SBT issuance flow. The animator's only interaction
  is with the PMC (Step~1); all subsequent steps are automated.}
  \label{fig:issuance_flow}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Work Recording:}
    The animator logs their daily work in the PMC as part of their normal workflow
    (cut assignments, status updates, completion timestamps).
    No additional effort is required for SBT issuance.
  \item \textbf{Milestone Trigger:}
    When a predefined milestone is reached (e.g., project completion, episode delivery,
    or manual request by the animator), the PMC sends a certification request
    to the Issuance Service.
  \item \textbf{Metadata Construction:}
    The Issuance Service aggregates the work log entries for the specified period,
    computes the \texttt{evidence\_hash}, constructs the JSON metadata, and uploads
    it to IPFS via a pinning service (e.g., Pinata).
  \item \textbf{Minting:}
    The Issuance Service calls the \texttt{mint()} function on the AnimatorSBT
    smart contract, passing the animator's wallet address and the IPFS content URI.
    In the production design, gas fees are covered by the Issuance Service
    through a meta-transaction relayer (ERC-2771);
    in the current prototype, the Issuance Service pays gas directly
    from its operational wallet.
  \item \textbf{Confirmation:}
    The PMC receives a transaction receipt and displays the newly issued SBT
    in the animator's profile, including a link to the Verification Portal.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Example scenario.}
Consider a freelance key animator, Tanaka, who is assigned 25 cuts
for Episode~7 of an anime series through a secondary subcontractor.
Over roughly ten weeks, Tanaka logs each cut assignment, status update,
and completion in the PMC as part of her normal workflow.
When Episode~7 wraps, the PMC triggers a certification request.
The Issuance Service aggregates Tanaka's work log
(role: \texttt{key\_anim}, task count: 25, period: 2025-01-15 to 2025-03-30),
computes the evidence hash, and mints an SBT to her embedded wallet.
Tanaka sees a new certificate in her PMC profile.
Three months later, when applying to a different studio,
she shares her wallet address.
The studio queries the blockchain and sees a tamper-evident, timestamped record
that Tanaka \emph{declared} 25 key animation cuts on a production during that
period. Absent a studio co-signature (\cref{subsec:cosigning}), this remains a
self-declaration rather than an independently confirmed fact---but it is stronger
evidence than an unverifiable r\'esum\'e line (it is timestamped, immutable, and
tied to a contemporaneous work log), and, combined with the usual interview and
portfolio review, it strengthens her case even though her name never appeared in
the episode's broadcast credits. Had the subcontractor co-signed the SBT, the same
record would rise to an independently verified credential.

% ----- 4.4 Verification Mechanism -----
\subsection{Verification Mechanism}
\label{subsec:verification}

Third parties can verify an animator's credentials through two methods:

\textbf{On-chain query:}
Given an animator's wallet address (or an Ethereum Name Service (ENS) name / human-readable identifier),
a verifier can query the AnimatorSBT contract's \texttt{balanceOf()} and
\texttt{tokenURI()} functions to retrieve all issued SBTs and their metadata.
Since the tokens are non-transferable, possession of an SBT at a given address
constitutes proof that the address holder earned the credential.

\textbf{Verification Portal:}
A web-based interface renders an animator's SBT portfolio in a human-readable format,
displaying project titles, roles, task counts, and time periods.
The portal can also recompute the \texttt{evidence\_hash} and, with the animator's
consent, compare it against the PMC's locally stored work logs.

It is important to state precisely what this check does and does not establish.
The \texttt{evidence\_hash} is a Keccak-256 hash of the serialized attributes;
recomputing it from the pinned metadata alone (as the static portal does,
\cref{subsec:prototype}) only detects whether the IPFS file was altered after
minting---a weak guarantee, since the issuer authored both the attributes and the
hash. Comparing it against the animator's \emph{local} work log is stronger: it ties
the on-chain certificate to a contemporaneous record. But even this establishes
only \emph{integrity} (the certificate corresponds to the work log that was hashed
at issuance), not \emph{authenticity} (that the work log reflects work actually
performed)---the log is self-reported. Moreover, the local-log comparison requires
the animator's consent and the continued existence of the local data, and is
therefore fragile to device loss. Authenticity requires the co-signing layer of
\cref{subsec:cosigning}.

% ----- 4.5 Privacy Considerations -----
\subsection{Privacy Considerations}
\label{subsec:privacy}

AnimatorSBT addresses privacy through several mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Minimal on-chain data:}
    Only the token ID, owner address, and IPFS URI are stored on-chain.
    All detailed metadata resides on IPFS.
    We caution, however, that in the current prototype this metadata---including
    \texttt{animator\_name}---is stored in \emph{cleartext} on public IPFS
    (see the example artifact in \cref{app:metadata}). The pseudonymity of the
    wallet address therefore does \emph{not} extend to the metadata: anyone with
    the URI can read the name and project details. Encrypting the metadata (or
    omitting directly identifying fields) is a recommended mitigation but is not
    the default today; we treat metadata-layer privacy as an open issue
    (\cref{subsec:legal}).
  \item \textbf{Nullable fields:}
    Sensitive information (studio name, project title) can be omitted
    when non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) apply, while still certifying role and task volume.
  \item \textbf{Selective disclosure:}
    The animator controls which SBTs are visible on the Verification Portal.
    Non-public SBTs can be shared via direct links with access control.
  \item \textbf{Embedded wallets:}
    By using account abstraction (ERC-4337), animators do not need to manage
    private keys directly, reducing the risk of key loss or social engineering attacks.
  \item \textbf{Erasure (a partial ``right to be forgotten''):}
    We are deliberately careful here, because blockchain immutability is in
    tension with erasure rights. Our \texttt{revoke()} function does \emph{not}
    burn the token or delete its \texttt{tokenURI}: by design it only sets a
    revocation flag, preserving the on-chain audit trail
    (\cref{subsec:contract}). A revoked token therefore remains queryable and its
    URI still resolves. Genuine erasure of the \emph{content} relies on unpinning
    the IPFS metadata, which renders it inaccessible through standard gateways---but
    any party that retained a copy can re-pin it, and the on-chain token, timestamp,
    and revocation record persist permanently. The practical mitigation for the
    PII this exposes is to encrypt the off-chain metadata and discard the key
    (``crypto-shredding''); we did not implement this in the prototype.
    We thus claim only a \emph{partial}, best-effort erasure capability, not a
    guarantee of forgetting, and flag the immutability-versus-APPI tension as
    unresolved in \cref{subsec:legal}.
\end{itemize}

% ----- 4.6 Co-signing / Attestation Design -----
\subsection{Co-signing and the Authenticity Layer}
\label{subsec:cosigning}

The mechanisms above produce a record that is \emph{tamper-evident}, timestamped,
and portable, but---as we are careful to repeat---authenticity (that the claimed
work was actually performed) is not established by issuance alone, because the
underlying work log is self-reported (\cref{subsec:trust}).
This subsection presents the design of an authenticity layer and a reference
implementation of it.
\textbf{We are precise about its status. The co-signing mechanism is \emph{not}
part of the contract currently deployed on Polygon Amoy (\cref{subsec:contract}),
which is the v1 contract exposing only \texttt{mint}, \texttt{mintBatch},
\texttt{revoke}, and view functions. It \emph{is}, however, implemented in a
separate reference contract, \texttt{AnimatorSBTV2}, with a passing test suite and
local gas measurements (\cref{subsec:prototype}). What remains undone is the part
that actually delivers authenticity: on-chain deployment, and operation by an
independent, identity-bound studio attester.}

\paragraph{A trust-level taxonomy.}
We distinguish three levels of credential strength, which the same SBT can move
through over time:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Self-attested} (the current prototype): minted from the animator's
    own work log. Establishes existence, integrity, and timestamp---stronger than
    an unverifiable claim, but not authenticated.
  \item \textbf{Peer-attested:} one or more co-workers on the same production
    endorse the claim, providing a social-verification signal short of an
    institutional one.
  \item \textbf{Studio-attested:} the contracting studio (or production manager)
    countersigns, converting the record into a mutually verified credential.
    This is the strongest level and the one most likely to satisfy a hiring
    verifier.
\end{enumerate}
A verifier can then weight a credential by its attestation level, and the
verification portal can surface that level explicitly.

\paragraph{Reference implementation.}
The realization reuses the contract's existing role-based access control
(\cref{subsec:contract}). \texttt{AnimatorSBTV2} adds a
\texttt{STUDIO\_ATTESTER\_ROLE} and the function
\texttt{coSign(uint256 tokenId)}, which records an attestation by
\texttt{msg.sender} against an existing token and emits \texttt{SBTCoSigned}.
We bind the attester to \texttt{msg.sender} rather than passing it as an argument
(as an earlier sketch did) so that the on-chain signature itself proves who
attested, with no spoofable parameter. The mechanism is idempotent (the same
attester cannot co-sign twice), supports multiple distinct studios co-signing one
token, and refuses to co-sign a revoked token. Companion views expose the result:
\texttt{attesters(tokenId)}, \texttt{attestationCount(tokenId)},
\texttt{hasCoSigned(tokenId, attester)}, and \texttt{attestationLevel(tokenId)}.
Studios are granted the attester role through the same admin pathway used for
issuers. A single \texttt{coSign} consumes approximately 95{,}000 gas in the local
EVM (\textasciitilde\$0.0006 at the baseline rate of \cref{tab:gas}); we report this
as a local-EVM figure only, since \texttt{AnimatorSBTV2} is not deployed on-chain
and, as \cref{subsec:contract} shows, local and on-chain gas can differ materially.
Two scope limits of the implementation are worth stating: \texttt{attestationLevel}
currently collapses the three-tier taxonomy above into a binary (\texttt{0} = no
studio co-signature, \texttt{1} = at least one), so the \emph{peer-attested} tier is
not separately distinguished on-chain; and the incentive design needed to motivate
studios to participate---which faces the transparency-resistance barrier of
\cref{subsec:adoption_barriers}---remains unaddressed.

\paragraph{The attester-independence caveat.}
The authenticity gain from co-signing is exactly as strong as the attester's
independence and real-world identity binding---neither of which the contract can
enforce on its own. Two gaps remain open. First, in the current single-operator MVP
the issuer wallet also holds \texttt{STUDIO\_ATTESTER\_ROLE} (and the issuance
service defaults the attester key to the issuer key), so a ``studio-attested'' token
can in fact be self-co-signed by the operator; this configuration adds no
authenticity and exists only to exercise the mechanism end to end. Second, even with
a distinct attester wallet, nothing \emph{on-chain} binds \texttt{STUDIO\_ATTESTER\_ROLE}
to a verified legal studio: the admin (TOONIQ) decides who is treated as a studio, so
co-signing shifts the root of trust from the animator's self-report to TOONIQ's
off-chain vetting of attesters rather than removing trust. Closing both gaps---an
independent attester operated by a real studio, with an auditable identity
binding---is what would convert a co-signed record from \emph{operator-attested} to
\emph{independently authenticated}, and is the primary item of future work
(\cref{sec:conclusion}). Honest framing of the present contribution is therefore
``a self-sovereign, tamper-evident, portable contribution \emph{ledger}, plus a
\emph{tested reference implementation} of the authenticity layer whose
\emph{independent operation} would make it a verified credential.''
