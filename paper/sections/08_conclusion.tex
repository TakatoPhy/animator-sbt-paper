% Conclusion and Future Work

% ----- Conclusion -----

This paper presented \textbf{AnimatorSBT}, a blockchain-based contribution
certification framework that addresses the systemic invisibility
of freelance animators' work in the Japanese anime industry.
By designing Soulbound Token issuance for integration
with an existing production management tool,
AnimatorSBT aims to create immutable, verifiable, and portable records
of individual contributions at the granular level of production tasks.

Our analysis identified a clear research gap:
while SBTs have been proposed and prototyped for healthcare, education, and agriculture,
and blockchain has been explored for creative IP management,
to the best of our knowledge no prior work addresses individual-level contribution
certification in anime production workflows.
We formalized the contribution invisibility problem,
defined five requirements for an ideal solution (completeness, verifiability,
persistence, portability, and minimal friction),
and assessed AnimatorSBT against all five. We find that it meets them by design,
with two important caveats made explicit throughout: \emph{verifiability} is
currently limited to integrity and existence---the authenticity of self-reported
claims requires a studio co-signing layer that we design and implement as a tested
reference contract (\cref{subsec:cosigning}) but do not yet deploy on-chain or
operate with independent studios---and the portability and minimal-friction claims
rest on wallet abstraction that is not yet built and remains to be validated with users.

A prototype---comprising the smart contract deployed on the Polygon Amoy testnet,
an Issuance Service, and a Verification Portal---demonstrates technical feasibility
and on-chain correctness, and we additionally provide a tested reference
implementation of the co-signing authenticity layer and deterministic evidence
hashing (60 passing tests), deployed locally but not yet on-chain. A cost analysis
using on-chain gas measurements indicates
that the \emph{blockchain layer} is not a cost barrier:
minting a single certificate costs approximately \$0.0013 on-chain, and the
dominant operating cost at scale is a flat IPFS pinning subscription (on the order
of \$240/year), not on-chain fees.
This supports the conclusion that Layer~2 scaling has removed the blockchain cost
barrier to credentialing; the broader operating costs (relayer subsidy, integration,
compliance) and, above all, the open adoption and authenticity questions remain
(\cref{subsec:eval_limitations}).

% ----- Future Work -----

Several directions for future work emerge from this research:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{User study (the most important next step):}
    Conduct a formal usability and adoption evaluation---the empirical validation
    this paper does not provide. We envisage a mixed-methods study with two
    populations: freelance animators (SBT recipients) and studios/production
    managers (potential verifiers and co-signers). Measures would include perceived
    value and trust, comprehension of what the credential does and does not prove,
    task-level usability of onboarding and certificate display (e.g., SUS and
    single-ease-question instruments), and stated willingness to adopt or co-sign.
    Method: task-based sessions with the integrated PMC prototype plus
    semi-structured interviews, ideally followed by a small field deployment.
    A reasonable success criterion would be that a majority of animator participants
    can correctly state the self-attestation caveat and complete issuance unaided,
    and that at least some studios express conditional willingness to co-sign.
    We report no such results here; this is a pre-registered-style plan, not an
    evaluation already performed.

  \item \textbf{Independent operation of the co-signing protocol:}
    We have implemented the two-party confirmation mechanism as a reference contract
    (\cref{subsec:cosigning}). The remaining---and decisive---work is to deploy it
    on-chain and operate it with \emph{independent, identity-bound} studio attesters
    (rather than the single-operator MVP in which the issuer also holds the attester
    role), to distinguish the peer-attested tier that the current binary
    \texttt{attestationLevel} collapses, and to design the incentives that motivate
    studios to participate despite the transparency they may prefer to avoid.

  \item \textbf{Interoperability with W3C Verifiable Credentials:}
    Extend AnimatorSBT to comply with the W3C Verifiable Credentials
    Data Model~2.0, enabling interoperability with the broader
    decentralized identity ecosystem and potential integration
    with government-issued digital identity systems.
    If SBT-based career records could be linked to national digital identity
    infrastructure, freelance animators might present institutionally recognized
    proof of professional activity. We flag this as contingent, not planned:
    direct use of the My Number identifier is tightly restricted by the My Number
    Act (\textit{Bang\=o H\=o}) to enumerated purposes, so the more plausible
    interface is the public-key authentication function (JPKI) rather than the
    My Number itself, and any such linkage would require legal review. Realized
    carefully, this could help shift an animator's status from undocumented
    independent worker toward one whose expertise is legible to banks, immigration
    authorities, and public institutions---an aspiration, contingent on the legal
    and co-signing prerequisites discussed above.

  \item \textbf{Cross-industry deployment:}
    Adapt the framework to adjacent creative industries
    (video games, film VFX, music production)
    to validate generalizability and identify domain-specific requirements.

  \item \textbf{On-chain analytics:}
    Develop aggregate analytics tools that leverage anonymized SBT data
    to study industry workforce dynamics---role distribution,
    project duration patterns, and career trajectories---providing
    data-driven insights for industry policy.

  \item \textbf{Integration with reputation systems:}
    Explore how accumulated SBTs can inform reputation scores
    that assist studios in identifying reliable freelancers,
    while preserving animator privacy through zero-knowledge proofs
    (e.g., ``this animator has completed $>$100 cuts across $>$5 productions''
    without revealing specific projects).

  \item \textbf{Contribution-based revenue sharing:}
    Existing blockchain initiatives in the anime industry
    (e.g., Animechain, Animecoin) have explored IP-level revenue distribution,
    but these systems rely on manually determined allocation percentages
    rather than verifiable contribution data.
    AnimatorSBT's on-chain contribution records could serve as
    the input layer for smart contract-based revenue sharing mechanisms,
    enabling automated royalty distribution proportional to verified work
    (e.g., secondary licensing revenue from merchandise or streaming).
    This extension would address the longstanding problem that anime creators
    receive no compensation from downstream IP monetization,
    despite their labor being the foundation of that value.
    We reiterate the regulatory caveat from \cref{subsec:legal}: attaching monetary
    entitlements to the token would change its legal classification analysis under
    the FIEA and Payment Services Act and would require dedicated legal review---it
    is precisely the step that moves the system out of the ``no monetary value''
    regime on which our preliminary classification rests.
\end{enumerate}

AnimatorSBT represents a step toward a more transparent and equitable
anime production ecosystem---one where every creative contribution
is recorded, tamper-evident, and owned by the person who made it,
and, with studio co-signing, independently verifiable.
As the anime industry continues its global expansion,
tools that aim to give individual creators a portable, tamper-evident record of
their work could become a valuable complement to institutional protections---contingent
on the co-signing and recognition prerequisites discussed above.
