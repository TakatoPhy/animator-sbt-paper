% Problem Statement

The anime production industry suffers from a systemic \textbf{contribution invisibility problem}:
a significant portion of the creative labor that produces anime goes unrecorded,
unattributed, and unverifiable.
This section formalizes the problem along three dimensions
and analyzes why existing solutions are insufficient.

% ----- 3.1 The Credit Gap -----
\subsection{The Credit Gap}
\label{subsec:credit_gap}

End credits are a curated subset rather than a log. Three things narrow them.
Work outsourced through successive tiers surfaces, if at all, as the
subcontracting company's name, so the individuals at the second or third tier
appear nowhere. Screen time and production-committee policy cap how many names
run at all. And an animator who leaves mid-production may be dropped despite
having finished cuts. \Cref{fig:cut_record} shows where this leaves the record:
the work is recorded in detail by the party that commissioned it, and the version
that reaches the public names a company.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/cut_record}
  \caption{The asymmetry this paper addresses. Work commissioned down a chain of
  subcontractors produces two records of who did it. The order the studio accepts
  names the person and the cuts it paid for, and stays inside the studio. The
  credits that reach the audience name a company. A co-signed certificate is a
  form of the first record that can cross the boundary.}
  \label{fig:cut_record}
\end{figure*}

% ----- 3.2 Consequences for Freelance Animators -----
\subsection{Consequences for Freelance Animators}
\label{subsec:consequences}

The credit gap has concrete consequences for the large share of animators
who work as freelancers (47.3\% in 2023~\cite{janica2023survey}, and 69.6\% in
2019~\cite{janica2019survey}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Portfolio asymmetry:}
    When seeking new contracts, freelancers must demonstrate their track record.
    Without credits, they rely on verbal claims, personal networks,
    or physical samples that lack independent verification.
    Studios hiring freelancers have no standardized way to verify
    a candidate's claimed experience.
  \item \textbf{Bargaining disadvantage:}
    Animators cannot leverage uncredited work in fee negotiations.
    A key animator who completed 30 cuts on a high-profile production
    but was not credited has no verifiable evidence to justify higher rates.
  \item \textbf{Career fragility:}
    Freelancers' career histories exist primarily in the memories
    of colleagues and in informal industry networks.
    When studios close---a frequent occurrence in the anime industry---
    or when key contacts retire, these informal records disappear.
  \item \textbf{Industry-level data loss:}
    At the aggregate level, the absence of comprehensive contribution records
    makes it difficult to study workforce dynamics, identify labor shortages
    in specific roles, or design effective industry policy.
    The surveys by JAniCA and NAFCA provide valuable snapshots
    but cannot capture project-level attribution data.
  \item \textbf{Barriers to social participation:}
    The consequences may extend beyond the industry.
    Mortgage applications, visa procedures, and bank credit assessments
    typically require documented proof of \emph{income} and stable employment---
    in Japan, instruments such as tax returns and withholding statements
    (\textit{gensen-ch\=osh\=uhy\=o}).
    A contribution record is not itself such a document: it attests to
    \emph{what work was done}, not to income, and institutions do not currently
    recognize on-chain records (\cref{subsec:legal}).
    We therefore do not claim that a contribution credential directly unlocks
    mortgages or visas. Rather, the absence of \emph{any} verifiable professional
    record is one contributing factor among several in animators' weak institutional
    standing; a portable attribution record could become a useful complement
    \emph{if} paired with co-signing (\cref{subsec:cosigning}) and integration
    with recognized institutional rails---a direction we treat as future work
    (\cref{sec:conclusion}), not a demonstrated capability.
\end{itemize}

% ----- 3.3 Limitations of Existing Solutions -----
\subsection{Limitations of Existing Solutions}
\label{subsec:existing_limitations}

Several recent initiatives have addressed aspects of the freelance animator problem,
but none fully resolves the contribution invisibility issue:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Freelance Protection Act (2024):}
    Japan's new legislation introduces protections for independent contractors,
    including written contract requirements and payment term regulations.
    However, it does not mandate contribution recording or attribution;
    an animator can have a legally compliant contract while still being uncredited.
  \item \textbf{NAFCA standard contracts:}
    NAFCA has developed template contracts for studio--animator agreements.
    While these improve transaction transparency,
    they are bilateral documents between two parties
    and do not create a publicly verifiable record
    that the animator can carry to future engagements.
  \item \textbf{Community databases (e.g., Sakugabooru, AniList):}
    Fan-maintained databases attempt to track animator contributions.
    These are valuable but rely on volunteer effort,
    are inherently incomplete, and lack official verification.
  \item \textbf{Centralized portfolio platforms:}
    Platforms such as ArtStation, and in Japan creator-portfolio services like
    foriio and Lancers, allow animators to showcase their work, but they depend on
    self-reported claims without third-party attestation or cryptographic proof,
    and they are controlled by a single operator that may change terms or shut down.
  \item \textbf{Production-management tools:}
    Animation/VFX production trackers---e.g., Autodesk Flow (ShotGrid), ftrack,
    the open-source Kitsu, and Japanese anime-specific tools such as Save Point
    and Anikuro---capture genuinely task-level data (per-shot/per-cut assignment
    and completion) as a byproduct of the workflow. However, that record is
    \emph{studio-owned}: it lives in the studio's instance, is not a third-party
    attestation the freelancer holds, and effectively disappears for the animator
    at offboarding or studio closure. They solve production tracking, not a
    portable, freelancer-owned credential.
\end{itemize}

% ----- 3.4 Problem Formalization -----
\subsection{Problem Formalization}
\label{subsec:formalization}

We define the contribution invisibility problem as follows.
Let $A$ be the set of all animators who contributed to a production $P$.
Let $C(P) \subseteq A$ be the set of animators credited in the official records of $P$.
The \textbf{credit gap} is:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta(P) = A \setminus C(P)
  \label{eq:credit_gap}
\end{equation}

For any animator $a \in \Delta(P)$, there exists no verifiable, persistent,
and portable record of their contribution to $P$.
We use this definition conceptually rather than as a computed quantity:
$\Delta(P)$ is not directly observable, since the uncredited set is by construction
unrecorded. A practical future proxy would compare, for a sample of productions, the
number of contributors recorded in a production-management tool against the names
appearing in those productions' broadcast credits; we propose this as a future
measurement (\cref{sec:conclusion}) and do not estimate $|\Delta(P)|$ here.

An ideal solution should satisfy the following requirements:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{R1}] \textbf{Completeness:} Every animator $a \in A$ can obtain a record,
    regardless of their position in the subcontracting hierarchy.
  \item[\textbf{R2}] \textbf{Verifiability:} Third parties can cryptographically verify
    the authenticity of the record without contacting the original studio.
  \item[\textbf{R3}] \textbf{Persistence:} The record survives studio closures,
    platform shutdowns, and changes in industry infrastructure.
  \item[\textbf{R4}] \textbf{Portability:} The animator owns and controls the record
    and can present it to any future employer or client.
  \item[\textbf{R5}] \textbf{Minimal friction:} The recording process must integrate
    into existing workflows without requiring additional effort from the animator.
\end{enumerate}

AnimatorSBT is designed to satisfy all five requirements, with one important
qualification recorded here and revisited in \cref{subsec:requirements_eval}:
for \textbf{R2 (Verifiability)} it currently provides only \emph{integrity and
existence} verification (that a claim was recorded, unaltered, at a given time),

operate with an independent studio; hence the $\triangle$ rather
than a \checkmark. R4 and R5 are satisfied by design but rest on wallet
abstraction that is not yet built and have not been validated with users.
